Effect of Internal Magnetic-Field Gradients
on Nuclear-Magnetic-Resonance
Measurements and
Nuclear-Magnetic-Resonance-Based
Pore-Network Characterization
Saurabh Tandon and Zoya Heidari, University of Texas at Austin
Summary
Nuclear-magnetic-resonance (NMR) measurements have been extensively used for determining porosity, pore-size distribution, and
ﬂuid saturation in porous media. However, internal gradients of the magnetic ﬁeld generated by the presence of paramagnetic or dia-
magnetic centers such as shale or clay particles can signiﬁcantly affect NMR response. Consequently, the resulting interpretation for
pore-size distribution and porosity is also affected. In this paper, we quantify the effect of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients and spatial
distribution of matrix components such as clay minerals on NMR response using pore-scale NMR numerical simulations. We also quan-
tify the inﬂuence of the aforementioned parameters on the NMR-based evaluation of porous media.
We used the ﬁnite-volume method to numerically solve the Bloch (1946) equations and simulated magnetization decay in porous
media. We cross validated the reliability of numerical simulations using analytical solutions given for spherical pores in different diffu-
sion regimes. The model was then used for simulation of NMR response in the pore-scale images of sandstone and carbonate rocks. We
used Larmor frequency of 2 MHz, external magnetic-ﬁeld gradient of 0.10 T/m, and half-echo spacing time of 0.5 ms for simulating
NMR response in pore-scale images of sandstones and carbonates. We then developed synthetic cases using actual rock images cover-
ing a wide range of spatial distribution of clay minerals (i.e., paramagnetic centers) to quantify the sensitivity of NMR decay to internal
magnetic-ﬁeld gradients. We quantiﬁed the sensitivity of NMR response for distribution of clays as thin laminae in the rock and as thin
layers on the surface of the grains.
The results showed that at low concentration (0.3 to 0.7%) of dispersed clay, there is a negligible effect of internal magnetic-ﬁeld
gradients on magnetization decay. At higher concentration of dispersed clay (5.1 to 7.3%), we observed a signiﬁcant effect of internal
magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on magnetization decay. The presence of shale minerals can cause 53% variation in the location of the trans-
verse-relaxation-time constant ( T
2) and up to 67% relative error in the assessment of dominant pore sizes. Shale laminations containing
clay were found to produce an effect of up to 31.8% on T2 relaxation-time constant, which could cause a relative error of 50.0% in esti-
mates of dominant pore size in the rock.
The outcomes of this paper demonstrated the effect of heterogeneous rock mineralogy on NMR response. The effect of internal
magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated by shale and clay on NMR becomes relevant when shale and clay particles are close to the pore ﬂuid
and their magnetic ﬁeld starts to affect the distribution of magnetic ﬁeld in the pore space. The results reveal the importance of
characterizing the distribution of shale and clay minerals before interpreting NMR response and can potentially improve conventional
techniques of pore-network characterization (pore-size distribution and pore volume) in the presence of clay minerals where internal
magnetic-ﬁeld gradients are not negligible.
Introduction
NMR studies of porous media have been used to obtain lithology-independent estimates of porosity, pore-size distribution, rock perme-
ability, and ﬂuid saturations (Toumelin et al. 2002, 2007; Mohnke and Klitzsch 2010; AlGhamdi and Arns 2012). NMR measurements
comprise applying magnetic ﬁelds and constant magnetic-ﬁeld gradients to quantify dephasing of proton spins in pore space (Torrey
1956; Bergman and Dunn 1995; Hu¨rlimann and Grifﬁn 2000). However, NMR response becomes complicated because of the presence
of susceptibility differences in pore ﬂuid and matrix components that produce internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients (Brown 1961; Dunn
2002; Mohnke and Klitzsch 2010; AlGhamdi and Arns 2012). Internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients distort the externally applied magnetic
ﬁelds (Dunn 2002; Sun and Dunn 2002). The effect of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on externally applied magnetic ﬁelds can be sig-
niﬁcant if paramagnetic or diamagnetic clays with high susceptibilities are present in the matrix (AlGhamdi and Arns 2012; Burcaw
and Callaghan 2012). Pore-scale simulation of NMR could help in quantifying the effects of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients, generated
by magnetic-susceptibility contrast, on NMR measurements and could help in improving NMR interpretation when this effect
is signiﬁcant.

Hu¨rlimann (1998) reviewed three different diffusion regimes (i.e., short time, motionally averaging, and localization) using the mag-
netization decay in a constant magnetic-ﬁeld gradient ﬁeld. He presented the criteria for distinguishing different diffusion regimes as
well as the analytical solutions for the magnetization decay for each of these regimes. Anand and Hirasaki (2007) studied the internal
magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated by a coating of paramagnetic particles on the surfaces of spheres using random-walk simulations.
AlGhamdi and Arns (2012) used the random-walk method to simulate NMR magnetization decay in pore-scale computed-tomography
(CT) images of both sandstone and carbonate-rock samples and included internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated by susceptibility
contrasts caused by both matrix components and paramagnetic particles present in the rock.
There is no systematic study on the effect of shale distribution in rocks on NMR response using micro-CT images of rock samples
documented in previous publications. Shale could be present in the rock matrix in the forms of structural shale, as dispersed (grain-
coating) shale on the surfaces of grains, or as shale laminations (Thomas and Stieber 1975; Moss 2008). The effect of shale minerals on
NMR measurements depends on the type of shale, its distribution, and pore-size distribution of the rock.
Hirasaki et al. (2001) studied the effect of clay on internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on North Burbank and Berea sandstones using
experiments as well as simulations. They used a spherical pore model and a simple model for generation of magnetic-ﬁeld gradients to
capture the effect of grain-coating clay particles covering the surface of the pores. They did not make any distinction between shale and
clay in their study. They concluded internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients derived from chlorite in North Burbank sandstone cause a more-
signiﬁcant error in NMR T
2 response than in Berea sandstone. Moss (2008) experimentally investigated the effect of increasing shale
on NMR measurements in different rock samples and documented the variation in NMR response caused by different conﬁgurations of
shale. However, the author did not explicitly discuss the effect of shale internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on T2 distribution.
AlGhamdi and Arns (2012) and Arns et al. (2011) modeled the NMR response in the presence of shale on micro-CT images of sand-
stones and carbonates. They also did not distinguish between shale and clay explicitly in their publications and did not investigate the
sensitivity of the NMR response to changes in shale concentration. Alhwety et al. (2014) investigated the effects of laminations, con-
trast in magnetic susceptibility of grains, and diffusional coupling for laminated systems using synthetic rock samples created with a
Boolean process. The work uses simulated rock samples with different pore-size distributions and then investigated the change in NMR
response with increasing values of magnetic susceptibility. Anand (2007) experimentally investigated the sensitivity of NMR to mag-
netic particles dispersed on the grain surfaces. In that work, silica grains are used as model sandstones. The silica-grain surfaces were
coated with magnetic particles by ﬂowing solutions with different concentrations of magnetic nanoparticles, and changes in NMR T
2
response were studied.
In this paper, we systematically study the effect of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated by matrix components including
grains and shale on NMR T2 distribution in sandstone and carbonate rocks. We numerically simulate NMR magnetization decay and
calculate T2 distribution in pore-scale images from sandstone and carbonate-rock samples. We then create synthetic examples using
real images with different spatial distributions of shale. The results of numerical simulations quantify the inﬂuence of the shale distribu-
tion on NMR interpretation results.
Method
This section describes the numerical-simulation method and the development of synthetic samples used for quantifying the effects of
shale distributions and internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on NMR measurements. We ﬁrst describe numerical simulation of the Bloch
(1946) and Torrey (1956) equations (Eq. 1) and veriﬁcation of the developed simulator. Then, we state the processing of micro-CT
images of sandstone and carbonate-rock samples and the development of synthetic cases with different types of shale distributions used
for sensitivity analysis. We ﬁnally simulate the NMR response in micro-CT images and synthetic samples to investigate the effects of
shale topology on NMR T
2 distribution.
Numerical Modeling of Bloch (1946) and Torrey (1956) Equations. We modiﬁed an existing ﬁnite-volume simulator OpenFOAM
(Weller et al. 1998) to numerically solve Eq. 1 with the boundary condition given in Eq. 7. The simulation code was veriﬁed by compar-
ing the estimated magnetization decay with the analytical solution given for a spherical pore provided by Brownstein and Tarr (1979)
and Kleinberg and Horsﬁeld (1990).
Sample Selection and Processing of Micro-CT Images. We simulated the NMR response for two sandstone (i.e., S1 and S2) and two
carbonate (i.e., C1 and C2) samples. In the case of Sample S1, Fiji, an open-source image-processing software (Schindelin et al. 2012)
was used to convert the original gray-scale micro-CT images to a binary image. Then, ImageJ, an open-source image-processing pro-
gram (Schindelin et al. 2012) developed at the National Institutes of Health, was used to reconstruct 3D rock matrices. The micro-CT
images of sandstone Sample S2 and carbonate Samples C1 and C2 were scanned and digitized by the Petroleum Engineering and Rock
Mechanics Group at Imperial College London (Dong 2007). Table 1 lists properties of rock samples used in the simulations. Figs. 1a
through 1d show the binary 3D micro-CT scan images of the four rock samples.

Synthetic samples with ideal distributions of shale in the pore space were generated from the available sandstone and carbonate
pore-scale images to investigate the effect of shale on NMR measurements. This paper includes the simulation results in two ideal
cases: (1) dispersed shale consisting of 60% volumetric concentration of clay and (2) shale laminations consisting of 60% volumetric
concentration of clay. To identify the effect of only internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated by clay minerals in shale on magnetiza-
tion decay, the susceptibility contrast between the diamagnetic grains, nonclay components of shale, and pore ﬂuids was assumed to be
negligible, and the presence of shale was assumed not to have any effect on the surface relaxivity of the rock grains.
Dispersed Shale. The ﬁrst synthetic case comprises a layer of dispersed (grain-coating) shale distributed on the surface of the grains.
The dispersed shale contains both paramagnetic clay and nonclay components. The dispersed shale is assumed to contain 60 vol% con-
centration of paramagnetic clay. The nonclay components of shale are assumed to have negligible magnetic-susceptibility contrast with
pore water and hence do not create any internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients. The changes in total rock porosity caused by interstitial poros-
ity of the dispersed shale have been assumed to be minimal (0.1 to 1.4%, 0.1 to 0.8%, 0.2 to 0.9%, and 0.1 to 0.7% for Samples S1, S2,
C1, and C2, respectively). The shale layer is also assumed to not signiﬁcantly affect the pore volume and the topology of the pore space
to isolate the effects of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on NMR measurements.
The thickness of the shale layer was assumed to increase from 0 to 5000 nm. To isolate the sensitivity of NMR T
2 measurements to
the thickness of the shale layer, we kept the composition of the shale layer constant for different shale-layer thicknesses. The volume of
each shale dipole was assumed to be proportional to the volume of shale present on the grain surface. Superposition of magnetic ﬁelds,
exerted by individual dipoles, provided the total magnetic ﬁeld in the pore space. The z-gradient of the total magnetic ﬁeld was used as
input for numerical simulation and was used for quantifying the effect of the internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on NMR measurements.

Laminated Shale. The second synthetic case comprises a shale lamina made of clay, small pores containing bound water, and nonclay
components in the rock. The volume of shale lamina increases from 5 to 20% of the volume of each rock sample. Fig. 2 shows the lam-
ina generated in Samples S1, S2, C1 and C2. Clay particles were assumed to be randomly distributed in the shale lamina. Shale lamina-
tions consisted of 60% volumetric concentration of clay. The clays in shale laminations are assumed to be randomly distributed.
The pores in lamina were also assumed to be randomly distributed. Each pore is an effective combination of many microsized pores
present in the shale sample. The surface relaxivity of the pores and the bulk-relaxation-rate constant were assumed to be 10 mm/s and 0.1
seconds, respectively (AlGhamdi and Arns 2012). We assumed that the water in shale pores is in a short-time diffusion regime and does
not undergo any dephasing caused by internal magnetic ﬁelds (Hu¨rlimann 1998). Laminations also introduced changes in the pore struc-
ture as well as in the porosity of the samples. Table 3 lists the change in sample porosity with increase in the volume of shale lamination.
Simulation of Magnetization Decay. The pore-scale images obtained from micro-CT and the synthetically generated samples were
inputs to the numerical simulator. Table 4 summarizes the assumed parameters used as inputs for numerical simulations of the Bloch
equations. The values assumed for the frequency of static magnetic ﬁeld (470 gauss), half-echo spacing time (0.5 ms), and external
magnetic-ﬁeld gradient (0.1 T/m) are within the typical range of values that are used for NMR logging and laboratory work (Prammer
et al. 1998; Freedman et al. 2003; Johnston et al. 2003; Toumelin et al. 2007; AlGhamdi and Arns 2012; Jarath, Torres-Verdin, and
Merletti, G. 2012; Alhwety et al. 2014; Boitel et al. 2015; Wolanski et al. 2015). The use of such high values for half-echo time (i.e.,
0.5 ms) and externally applied magnetic-ﬁeld gradient provides an insight into the effect of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradient on different
experiments and pulse sequences that use high static magnetic ﬁelds or different magnetic-gradient strengths and echo times. Examples
of such cases include pulse-ﬁeld-gradient sequences (Tanner 1965) and diffusion-editing sequence (Flaum 2007).
For simulations, it was assumed that the initial wait time was large enough for complete initial buildup of magnetization. Then, a
90
y pulse was applied that rotated magnetization into the transverse plane. The initial 90 y pulse was followed by a series of 180 x pulses
to generate the CPMG sequence. Each 180 x pulse was assumed to completely rotate the magnetization with respect to the x-axis. For
a very small timestep, large pore radii, and small echo-spacing time, the rock pores could be assumed to be in free-diffusion regime
(Hu¨rlimann 1998). The formulation provided by Torrey (1956) and Carr and Purcell (1954) remains valid, and magnetization decay
caused by internal or external magnetic-ﬁeld gradients could be simulated using Eq. 10. The overall magnetic-ﬁeld gradients in the
rock were modeled through superposition of externally applied magnetic-ﬁeld gradients and internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated
by susceptibility contrasts between rock matrix components and pore ﬂuids.
Inversion of Magnetization Decay. T
2 distribution was obtained by inversion of magnetization decay using the Levenberg-Marquardt
ﬁtting method and the zeroth-order curve-ﬁtting scheme (Dunn et al. 2002; Aster et al. 2005). The inversion method used for obtaining
T2 distribution from magnetization decay is described by Medellı´n et al. (2016).
Results
Code Validation. The simulations were ﬁrst validated by simulating magnetization decay for a spherical pore with bulk- and surface-
relaxation effects and no magnetic-ﬁeld gradients. Fig. 3 shows the comparison of simulated magnetization decays for spherical pores of
different pore radii against the analytical solution provided in Brownstein and Tarr (1979) for this simpliﬁed case. The radius of the
spherical pore, Rs, was increased from 10 to 150 mm to cover different relaxation regimes deﬁned by Brownstein and Tarr (1979). Appen-
dix A describes the analytical solution for this simpliﬁed case. In the case of a single spherical pore with surface relaxivity of 5 mm/s and
transverse bulk-relaxation rate of 2.5 seconds, the difference between the results from the numerical simulator and the analytical method
in all cases was less than 5%. Each simulation took a few minutes to run on a desktop computer with a single core and 8 GB RAM.
We then validated the simulation results by simulating magnetization decay for a spherical pore under a constant magnetic-ﬁeld gra-
dient with CPMG echo sequence and no surface effects. The radius of the spherical pore, Rs, was increased from 10 to 150 mm. Neuman
(1974) and Kleinberg and Horsﬁeld (1990) discussed the analytical solution for this simple case. The analytical solution is presented in
Appendix B. Fig. 4 shows the comparison of simulated magnetization decays with the analytical solution. For each of these cases, the
externally applied magnetic-ﬁeld gradient is assumed to be 0.1 T/m and the half-echo time ( s) is assumed to be 0.0005 seconds. The
90y pulse and 180 x pulses in the CPMG echo sequence are assumed to perfectly rotate the magnetization vector. The pores are assumed
to have reﬂecting walls and a ﬂuid bulk decay constant of 2.5 seconds. The error in each case is found to be less than 5%.

excellent agreement between the simulated and the analytical solution in all the cases.

Discussions
Table 5 provides a summary of results for dominant peak sizes obtained using simulations for different distributions of magnetic-ﬁeld
gradients discussed in this text. External magnetic-ﬁeld gradients in a sample cannot be avoided because they are the nature of the log-
ging tool or inside-out spectrometer used for making NMR measurements. The effects of external gradients are more signiﬁcant
because they affect how a proton spins homogeneously in the entire pore space and lead to increased proton dephasing.
We observed no change in T2 peak caused by the presence of diamagnetic centers in quartz and calcite grains. Similar results are
also seen in other studies, including Hirasaki et al. (2001), Flaum (2007), and Anand and Hirasaki (2007), which found a limited effect
of internal gradients in both carbonates and clay-free Bentheimer sandstone samples during experiments.
We observed this minimal change because quartz and calcite grains have magnetic susceptibilities of /C0 16.41/C2 10/C0 6 vol/vol and
/C0 13.1/C2 10/C0 6 vol/vol, respectively, which are very close to magnetic susceptibility of water (i.e., /C0 9.03/C2 10/C0 6 vol/vol) in the pore
space. Furthermore, the diamagnetic centers that are in the middle of the grains do not signiﬁcantly inﬂuence internal gradients because
the generated internal magnetic-ﬁeld decay is inversely proportional to the cube of distance away from the diamagnetic centers.

We observed a gradual decrease in dominant T2 peak as the thickness of the dispersed-shale layer increases. Similar decreases in the
dominant T2 peak were obtained for silica grains in the experimental work of Anand (2007) and Anand and Hirasaki (2007). The effects
of internally generated magnetic-ﬁeld gradients are usually local. A layer of magnetic centers on the surface of grains creates large mag-
netic-ﬁeld gradients in their vicinity. The changes in T
2 peaks are gradual because most of the centers are close to the pore ﬂuid and the
magnitude of gradient generated depends only on the concentration of magnetic particles.
In addition to internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients, the presence of the dispersed shale in the rock causes a shift in T2 distribution by
changing the pore-size distribution and blocking of pores and pore throats; creating variations in surface relaxivity and surface/volume
ratios of shale dispersed on the grains; and imposing diffusive coupling of large grain pores with pores in the shale sample. It is possible
that the previously discussed mechanisms completely mask the effect of internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients generated by paramagnetic
clays. This study isolates the effect of only internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on NMR response in the rock samples. We demonstrate
that the shift in T
2 distribution caused by magnetic-ﬁeld gradients can be signiﬁcant in rocks with high concentrations of paramagnetic
clays, such as the case of North Burbank sandstone, which was previously documented by Hirasaki et al. (2001) and Anand (2007).
The decrease in the dominant T2 peak was more abrupt for the cases of laminated shale. Similar results were obtained by Alhwety
et al. (2014) using synthetic rock samples. We observed abrupt changes because the magnetic centers are assumed to be randomly dis-
tributed in the shale layer. In the case of laminated shale, the shift in the T2 distribution and the change in rock porosity depend on the
location of the lamination and its proximity to the pores. This mechanism is illustrated in Figs. 10 and 11, which show the effect of the
presence of shale lamination in Samples C1 and C2, respectively. We can clearly see that shale lamination intersected some of the pores
in Samples C1 and C2, decreasing their overall porosity. In Sample C2, the shale lamination is also situated close to other pores in the
sample, and hence causes movement of the T
2 peak. On the other hand, the pores in Sample C1 are situated farther away from the shale
lamination. Hence, the movement in the peak is minimal when the relative volume of the lamination is 5% but there is movement in the
peak when the volume increases to 20%. Consequently, even if the concentration of magnetic centers increases, their inﬂuence on the
T
2 peak might be limited when they are situated away from the pore ﬂuid.
The results of numerical simulations demonstrated the importance of identifying the distribution of magnetic-ﬁeld gradients during
NMR interpretation. Externally applied magnetic-ﬁeld gradients are usually a characteristic of the spectrometer used for the measure-
ment of NMR response. The effects of these gradients can be mitigated using shorter echo spacing and better tools with smaller exter-
nally applied gradients. The effects of internally generated gradients are more difﬁcult to identify and mitigate. As demonstrated in this
text, the effects of internally generated gradients depend on shale type, topology, and concentration of magnetic particles. These effects
will be accentuated if the shale is dispersed and if it contains high concentrations of paramagnetic clays such as chlorite and illite. Accu-
rate assessment of shale concentration and its magnetic properties is required for reliable interpretation of NMR T
2 measurements. If it
is suspected that internal magnetic-ﬁeld gradients might be affecting the NMR T2 response, or if the magnetic properties of shale are
uncertain, then using tools with smaller echo spacing times or using T1 response provides a more-reliable method for assessment of
rock properties. Use of pore-scale simulation of NMR presents another possible method of obtaining accurate estimates of rock proper-
ties in such cases.

The results of numerical simulations depend strongly on echo spacing, initial static magnetic ﬁeld, and the external magnetic-ﬁeld
gradients used in the NMR numerical simulations. The results shown in this paper are applicable to the speciﬁc range of simulation
parameters used in this publication. These results might not cover characteristics of all the NMR logging tools and experimental setups.
It should also be noted that in our model the pores are assumed to be in a free-diffusion regime. Although this is a valid assumption for
most of the pores encountered in sandstones and carbonates, it might cause errors in magnetic-decay calculations in extremely small
pores. Shale, clay minerals, and nonclay minerals are also modeled as dipoles using the effective-medium theory, which introduces the
limitation of sharp decay in a magnetic ﬁeld away from the dipole. In addition, the micro-CT imaging process contains the drawback of
not resolving pores that are smaller than the resolution of the equipment. Finally, we are sampling small volumes of the rocks for imag-
ing and meshing. In the case of highly heterogeneous rocks, such as carbonates containing vugs and micropores, the pore-scale samples
selected for simulation might not represent the conﬁguration of pore space in the formation. Selection of a larger sample size, if compu-
tational capabilities allow, can help in capturing pore-scale variations in heterogeneous rocks.
Conclusions
We quantiﬁed the effects of externally applied magnetic-ﬁeld gradients and internally induced static magnetic ﬁelds caused by
diamagnetic grains or dispersed/laminated-shale distributions on NMR T
2 distribution using pore-scale numerical simulations. To inves-
tigate the variation of magnetic-ﬁeld gradients on different rock samples, we used two sandstone samples and two carbonate samples.
The grains and clay particles were modeled as dipoles distributed on the grain surface, inside the rock grains, or within shale lamina-
tions. We used a theoretical effective-medium model developed for dipoles in a constant magnetic ﬁeld to investigate the effects of
the grain/pore-ﬂuid and clay/pore-ﬂuid susceptibility contrast on NMR response. We observed signiﬁcant changes in NMR T
2 distribu-
tion in the presence of dispersed and laminated magnetic shale and observed minimal changes caused by diamagnetic quartz and
calcite grains.
This paper illustrated the use of pore-scale simulations in developing a fundamental understanding of the behavior of NMR in situa-
tions where internal gradients generated by susceptibility contrast cannot be ignored. It is generally accepted that the dominant peak of
the NMR T2 distribution represents the dominant pore size of the rock; however, we demonstrated that the presence of signiﬁcant inter-
nal gradients could lead to an underestimation in the size of dominant pores, pore-size distribution, and rock permeability using NMR-
based formulations such as the Timur-Coates model (Coates, G. R., Xiao, L., and Prammer, M. G. 1999).
The outcomes of this paper also help in understanding and quantifying the inﬂuence of internal gradients caused by the presence of
clay minerals and their spatial distribution on NMR measurements, and in improving real-time NMR-based assessment of porosity and
pore-size distribution in the presence of signiﬁcant internal gradients.
