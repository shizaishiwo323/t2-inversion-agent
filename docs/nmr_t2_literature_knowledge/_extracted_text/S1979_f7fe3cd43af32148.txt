Magnetization Evolution in Network Models of Porous Rock
under Conditions of Drainage and Imbibition
D. Chang and M. A. Ioannidis1
Porous Media Research Institute, Department of Chemical Engineering, University of Waterloo, Waterloo, Ontario N2L 3G1, Canada
Received October 3, 2001; accepted May 9, 2002
Surface-enhanced relaxation of nuclear magnetization in fully
and partially saturated water-wet porous media is studied using a
pore network simulator. The simulator is based on a description
of the pore space in terms of a regular cubic lattice of pores and
throats following respective size distributions. The latter are ob-
tained from geometric characterization of 3D stochastic replicas of
real porous rock samples. Concepts of percolation theory are used
to simulate the pore-scale distribution of a strongly wetting phase
under conditions of quasistatic drainage by or imbibition against a
nonwetting phase. The results of these simulations compare favor-
ably with experimental mercury intrusion/retraction curves. The
equations governing magnetization evolution in a connected pore
system are then solved by matrix diagonalization for different val-
ues of wetting-phase saturation along the primary drainage and
secondary imbibition paths. Analysis of the corresponding spec-
tra of decay rates provides new insights regarding the inﬂuence of
pore structure (pore and throat size distributions, spatial correla-
tion), surface relaxation strength, and ﬂuid distribution on diffusive
coupling between pores. For pore and throat size distributions and
surface relaxation strength representative of sandstones, diffusive
coupling is quite important, especially under conditions of partial
saturation. Remarkably, simulations show that correlated hetero-
geneity is the main reason pores appear poorly coupled with respect
to NMR relaxation—an assumption underlying the correspondence
between pore size and relaxation time distributions. Finally, for a
given value of wetting-phase saturation, the history of saturation
change (drainage or imbibition) is shown to have a profound effect
on the spectrum of decay rates.

1. INTRODUCTION
The sensitivity of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) to
molecular-level events within ﬂuids provides important oppor-
tunities for determining ﬂuid states and properties within porous
media (1). NMR methods have recently found important prac-
tical applications in downhole well logging for hydrocarbon
formation evaluation (2). In a NMR measurement, polarized

The interpretation of magnetization decay observed in porous
rock is based on two fundamental, and seemingly contradictory,
assumptions (2). The ﬁrst assumption is that each pore is in the
“fast-diffusion” regime; i.e., diffusion is fast enough to maintain
a uniform magnetization across space. This assumption is es-
sential for establishing a direct relationship between relaxation
time and pore size, as shown in Eq. [1]. Sufﬁciently large pores
may be in the slow-diffusion regime, although in such cases it
is likely that the longer surface relaxation will be swamped by
T

relaxation time distributions that were independent of tempera-
ture over the range 25 –175

diffusion-limited case depends explicitly on the bulk diffusion
coefﬁcient (cf. Eq. [1]), itself a function of temperature, the re-
sults of Latour et al. (6) suggest that the samples studied were
in the fast-diffusion regime.
The second assumption is that diffusion between pores is
unimportant, so that magnetization in each pore decays indepen-
dently of other pores. On this assumption, the broad distribution
of relaxation times usually observed in samples of porous rock

Following Cohen and Mendelson (10), McCall et al. (11)
investigated the effects of diffusive coupling between pores un-
der conditions of fast diffusion. These authors formulated and
solved the coupled magnetization balance equations for a fully
saturated network of pores connected with pore throats. They
demonstrated that the breadth of the decay rate distribution de-
creases and the distribution shifts to higher rates, as coupling
increases. For very strong coupling, the spectrum is reduced to a
narrow peak at a rate determined by the ratio of the total surface
to total volume of the pore system. This situation corresponds
to a magnetization that is uniform within the entire sample and
decays as a single exponential.
McCall et al.(11) argued that pore coupling in sandstone rocks
is weak, and therefore the assumption that the spectrum of decay
rates reﬂects the density of pore sizes is probably a good one.
Their conclusions, however, were based on simulations using
random pore networks in which the size of pore throats was
uniform. In real sandstone rock and other porous media, not
only pore and throat sizes follow respective distributions, but
also correlation between the size of a throat and the size of
adjacent pores (12, 13), as well as spatial correlation among
the pore sizes (14 –16), are very likely to exist. None of these
correlations were included in the model of McCall et al. (11).
Their theoretical prediction that a signi ﬁcant narrowing of the
decay rate spectrum should take place as the coupling strength
is increased by increasing the bulk diffusion coefﬁcient (i.e., by
increasing temperature) was not con ﬁrmed by the experiments
of Latour et al. (6).
Importantly, the assumption that a relaxation time distribution
corresponds to a pore size distribution also underlies attempts to
estimate a drainage capillary pressure curve by simply rescal-
ing a relaxation time distribution (17). This correspondence has
received little theoretical attention. NMR relaxation measure-
ments in porous samples that have undergone drainage-type
displacement have revealed qualitative changes in the spectrum
of decay rates, which are consistent with the accepted view that
(i) relaxation time is proportional to pore size for water-saturated
pores and (ii) the nonwetting ﬂuid enters progressively smaller
pores as the water saturation decreases (4).
Network models have been instrumental in understanding
the distribution and ﬂow of immiscible ﬂuids in porous media
(18, 19). However, with the exception of McCall et al. (11),
who only considered fully saturated networks of very simple
and somewhat unrealistic geometry, NMR relaxation in porous
media has not been studied by network simulation. This task is
undertaken here. We are particularly interested in network mod-
els which are consistent with structural information extracted by
analysis of 3D images of the pore space and which can quan-
titatively describe experimental mercury porosimetry data (20).
With such models at hand, our attention is then focused on infor-
mation about the pore geometry and ﬂuid distribution that may
be garnered by NMR relaxation analysis.
This paper is structured as follows. In theﬁrst section we de-
scribe the construction of the pore network model, paying partic-
ular attention to its geometric and correlation aspects (pore and
throat size distributions, pore/throat correlation, spatial struc-
ture, pore surface roughness). In the same section we brieﬂy de-
scribe the algorithms used to simulate drainage and imbibition,
develop the magnetization balance equations, and discuss their
solution. In a subsequent section, we ﬁrst demonstrate that the
network model can successfully simulate experimental mercury-
air drainage and imbibition capillary pressure curves obtained
from Berea sandstone and North Sea chalk samples. We then an-
alyze magnetization decay spectra obtained at different values
of saturation along the primary drainage and secondary imbibi-
tion paths. We show that, in the absence of spatial correlation
among the pores and for pore structure and relaxation parameters
consistent with experimental data for Berea sandstone, diffusive
coupling is quite pronounced. We then demonstrate that spatial
correlation of the pore sizes masks the effects of diffusive cou-
pling, so that the observed spectrum of decay rates at S
w = 1
corresponds well with the pore size distribution, even when dif-
fusive coupling is signiﬁcant. We also discuss the signi ﬁcance

2. THEORY
2.1. Network Model of Rock Pore Structure
Following previous work (21, 22), we choose to represent the
structure of porous rock with a regular cubic lattice of pores and
throats. Pores are identiﬁed with sites in the lattice and throats
with bonds between sites. We consider that pores are irregular
in shape and choose to characterize them by an equivalent-cube
dimension, R
p, so that their pore volume and surface area are
given byVp = 8R3
p and Sp = 24R2
p, respectively. We also assume
that pore sizes are randomly distributed on lattice sites according
to a known pore size distribution (PSD), in an uncorrelated or
spatially correlated fashion. In the latter case, we choose an
isotropic exponential autocorrelation function with correlation
length equal to ﬁve lattice sites (15, 22).
Consistent with recent advances in 3D pore space character-
ization (23, 24), we take pore throats to represent volumeless
constrictions between pores. The throat cross sections may also
be irregular. We choose to characterize pore throats in terms
of a single dimension, R
t , such that Rt = √At /2, where At is
the throat cross-sectional area. The distributions of both At and
Vp can be measured, if a description of the pore space in the
form of 3D volume data is available (23, 24). Determination of
the characteristic dimensions R
p and Rt from such information
is straightforward. Throat sizes from a respective distribution
(TSD) are distributed on the bonds of the lattice according to
the bond-site correlation scheme detailed elsewhere (21, 25).
This scheme introduces positive correlation between the size
of a throat and the size of the pores communicating through
it, in agreement with the generally accepted view of rock pore
structure (13). To account for resistance to molecular diffusion
between pores in the model, we consider an interpore diffu-
sion length distribution (DLD). Interpore diffusion lengths cor-
respond to the lengths of distinct pore space channels. Their
determination by morphological skeletonization of 3D volume
data has been detailed elsewhere (23). Interpore diffusion lengths
are also positively correlated to the size of the adjoining pores.
Pore size, throat size, and interpore length distributions obtained
from 3D stochastic models representative of Berea sandstone are
shown in Fig. 1.

2.3. Modeling the Effect of Irregular Pore Geometry
on Fluid Distribution
It is important to note that the rock-pore interface in real
porous media is quite rough and often fractal (32–34). Accord-
ingly, pore and throat sizes may be expected to span a broad
range of length scales. In agreement with this expectation, ex-
perimental mercury porosimetry curves usually reveal that con-
siderable fractions of the total pore volume are intruded over
a very broad range of pressures. These observations have two
important consequences for the simulation of drainage and im-
bibition using network models. First, PSD and TSD functions
determined by analysis of 3D volume data of limited resolution
(ca. 1–5 µm for sandstone and 0.1–0.3 µm for chalk) and subse-
quently used in network models do not represent the entire spec-
trum of pore and throat sizes present in real rock. Secondly, even
if the pore volume distribution were perfectly known, mapping
of irregular pores (see Fig. 2) onto regular, albeit angular, objects
(e.g., cubes) would still result in poor approximation of the vol-
ume of the wetting ﬂuid that remains in the pores after they are
invaded by a nonwetting ﬂuid. Tsakiroglou and Payatakes (35)
recognized this and proposed to endow network models with a
geometrically determined fractal model of the pore surface, in
connection with simulations of mercury porosimetry.
Here, we propose to capture the effect of pore structure that
is not explicitly represented in the network model, on the dis-

3.2. Decay Rate Spectra of Fully Saturated
Model Pore Networks
Having established a pore network model capable of describ-
ing the drainage and imbibition capillary pressure curves of a
real sample, we examined NMR relaxation atS
w = 1. Using the
parameters corresponding to network 3D-SS3U (see Table 1),
we carried out simulations usingρ = 100 µm/s (high surface re-
laxivity) and ρ = 5 µm/s (low surface relaxivity). These values
are roughly at the extremes of reported surface relaxivity values,
with ρ = 5 µm/s frequently attributed to sandstones (17, 39). In
each case, we compared the true decay rate spectrum to the one
obtained if diffusion between the pores is ignored (i.e.,ω
c = 0).
These comparisons are shown in Fig. 6a ( ρ = 100 µm/s) and
Fig. 6b (ρ = 5 µm/s). For ρ = 100 and 5 µm/s, the mean relax-
ation times are τo = 0.0684 and 1.37 s, respectively. The ratio of
the two is precisely equal to the ratio of surface relaxivities, as
expected. For Berea sandstone samples, Latour et al. (6) have
reported mean relaxation times in the range 0.2 –0.5 s for tem-
peratures in the range 25–175
◦C. Examination of Fig. 6 reveals
that, for a high value of ρ, the effects of pore coupling on the
spectrum of decay rates appear to be small, whereas for a low
value ofρ, the spectrum is reduced to a sharp peak. The latter re-
sult is in qualitative accord with the predictions of McCallet al.
(11), for situations corresponding to ω
c ≫ ωρ .I f ρ = 5 µm/s is
representative of sandstones, however, our computations show
that mangetization decay should be dominated by diffusive cou-
pling, something which has not been observed experimental-
ly (6).
Remarkably, spatial correlations mask the effects of pore cou-
pling on the decay rate spectra. This is born out by comparing
the magnetization decay in 2D pore networks without and with
spatial correlations (networks 2D-SS3U and 2D-SS3C, respec-
tively) for a value of ρ = 5 µm/s. These results are shown in
Fig. 7a for 2D-SS3U and Fig. 7b for 2D-SS3C. In the absence
of spatial correlation the results are similar to those obtained
from 3D networks (see Fig. 6a), as strong diffusive coupling
signiﬁcantly reduces the breadth of the spectrum. The effect is
somewhat weaker in 2D because of reduced pore connectivity,
as also noted by McCallet al. (11). On the contrary, when pores
of similar size are clustered together, exchange of magnetization
by diffusion between pores cannot effectively homogenize the
kinetics of surface-enhanced relaxation (see Fig. 7b). Note the
presence of a number of small peaks in the spectrum shown in
Fig. 7b. Coupling is not weak but localized, and therefore its
effects are not observable in the presence of spatial structure

3.3. Decay Rate Spectra of Partially Saturated
Model Pore Networks
Network Model 3D-SS3U was used to study NMR relaxation
under conditions of drainage and imbibition. This network lacks
spatial structure and, on the basis of the observations made in the
previous section, it is not entirely representative of Berea sand-
stone. Since we could not investigate NMR relaxation in large
three-dimensional spatially correlated pore networks, we chose
to study the problem using Model 3D-SS3U withρ = 100 µm/s.
Under such conditions, the effects of pore coupling appear to be
weak (see Fig. 6a).
As seen in Fig. 9a, the larger pores are preferentially ﬁlled
with nonwetting ﬂuid during drainage. Note that we do not con-
sider bulk relaxation effects and therefore no signal from the
nonwetting phase is reported in the spectra. The wetting ﬂuid
in the invaded pores continues to be in contact with the entire
surface of the pores, but its volumetric content decreases dur-
ing drainage. This results in the gradual disappearance of the
slower decay rates as the effective surface to volume ratio of the
invaded pores increases, and the spectrum shifts toward higher
rates. The remaining slow decay rates present in the spectrum
belong to larger pores that are inaccessible by the nonwetting
ﬂuid.
During imbibition, the smaller pores are preferentially in-
vaded by the wetting ﬂuid, resulting in the gradual disappear-
ance of the faster decay rates, as shown in Fig. 9b. The spec-
trum gradually shifts to slower rates as an increasingly greater
amount of wetting phase exists in larger pores. As the nonwetting
phase saturation decreases, the spectrum of decay rates shown in
Fig. 9a for S
w = 1 is only partly recovered, due to the presence
of residual nonwetting-phase saturation. This is more clearly
shown in Fig. 10a. Note the presence of a second peak in the
spectrum under conditions of residual nonwetting-phase satu-

4. CONCLUSIONS
Network modeling was employed to study NMR relaxation
under conditions of drainage and imbibition in porous media. A
novel aspect of this work is that the model pore networks stud-
ied were developed using microstructure information obtained
by comprehensive geometric and topological characterization
of 3D stochastic replicas of sandstone and chalk samples. The
effects of unresolved pore space geometry on ﬂuid distribution
were taken into account using a simple but physically mean-
ingful two-parameter model. This enabled us to model quanti-
tatively the measured mercury-air drainage and imbibition cap-
illary pressure curves of Berea sandstone and North Sea chalk
samples. Simulations of magnetization evolution in pore net-
works revealed that, for pore structure and surface relaxation
parameters representative of sandstones, spin diffusion between
pores results in signiﬁcant narrowing of the decay rate spectrum,
unless spatial correlation among pore sizes is taken into account.
This result offers an explanation for the apparently contradictory
experimental observation of broad relaxation time distributions
under conditions of fast diffusion in water-saturated reservoir
rock samples. We therefore suggest that such observations are
evidence of correlated heterogeneity in porous rock.
Additionally, we used the simulator to explore the relaxation
response of partially saturated porous rock. The results reveal
the appearance in the drainage spectra of decay rates faster than
any of the rates observed at S
w = 1. These rates were seen to
correspond to the decay of magnetization in partially drained
small pores. We suggest that similar observations in experimen-
tal systems may be taken as an indication of diffusive coupling of
magnetization between macro- and microporosity. Furthermore,
it was observed that relaxation spectra at partial saturation are
very sensitive to saturation history (drainage vs imbibition). It
was also shown that diffusion of magnetization between pores
precludes the identi ﬁcation of pores containing trapped oil in
the spectrum of decay rates at S
or . Finally, the connections be-
tween drainage capillary pressure curves and relaxation spectra
at S
w = 1 were explored.
