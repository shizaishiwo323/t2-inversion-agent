4.1 NMR in porous media 
As described in earlier chapters, the nuclear magnetic relaxation behavior of 
water in porous media tends to be quite different from that of water in the 
bulk phase. There were experiments [41, 37] performed which demonstrated 
that water, when it resides in the pore space of a rock (e.g. Berea sandstone), 
has a much shorter relaxation time (T1 typically varies from 10 to several 
hundred milliseconds) than it has in the bulk phase (about 3 seconds). The 
very same water, when removed from the rock, regains its long relaxation 
time. Hence, the water that came into contact with the Berea sandstone 
did not change, or at least did not change significantly enough to have any 
discernible change in the relaxation time. Accordingly, one can only interpret 
the fast relaxation of water in Berea sandstones as the result of enhanced 
surface relaxation at the pore-matrix interface, which is presumably due to 
a combination of paramagnetic impurity ions and reduced correlation times 
at the interface. 
In porous rocks, we mostly study the relaxation of hydrogen nuclei in the 
pore space. The values of T~ and T2 are affected by the physical and chemical 
properties of the rock fluids, the uniformity of the the static magnetic field, 
the variations in pore sizes, the differences between the magnetic susceptibil- 
ity of pore fluids and rock grains, the fluid viscosity and diffusion, and the 
presence of paramagnetic impurity ions on the pore walls or in the fluids.

4.1.1 Surface relaxation 
The basic physics of NMR relaxation of fluids at solid surfaces in porous me- 
dia were first studied by Korringa, Seevers, and Torrey [129]. They identified 
two surface relaxation mechanisms: one occurs at all sites on the surface, and 
the other is associated with dilute paramagnetic ion impurities. The relax- 
ation process associated with all non-magnetic sites on the surface is much 
too weak to account for the fast relaxation observed for water in rocks (A1- 
magor and Belfort [6], D'Orazio et al. [73]). Para- and ferromagnetic ions, 
such as iron, manganese, nickel, and chromium can cause strong surface re- 
laxation. Their presence on the solid surfaces of the rock will dominate the 
relaxation process of water in the pore space. Typical sandstones have about 
1% iron content, and some contain a significant concentration of manganese. 
This level of paramagnetic impurity ions will make spins relax very efficiently. 
Carbonates, on the other hand, have more varied mineralogies, large vuggy 
pores, and a wider range of surface relaxation strength (typically smaller 
than sandstones). Hence, their relaxation times can sometimes be as long as 
the bulk water values. 
Fast, intermediate, and slow diffusion regimes 
There is evidence that the relaxation of polarized proton spins in fluids, which 
occupy porous rocks or biological tissues, cannot be described by a single re- 
laxation time. Brownstein and Tarr [45] demonstrated that multiexponential 
decay arises simply as a consequence of an eigenvalue problem of spin dif- 
fusion in porous media of various sizes and shapes, even in the case of a 
very simple shape of an isolated void or biological cell. They considered two 
properties which govern the evolution of spin polarization in the system: (1) 
paramagnetic materials on the solid/fluid interface acting as relaxation cen- 
ters, and (2) classical diffusion of water molecules with the diffusion constant 
characteristic of bulk water.

In the fast diffusion regime, there is a large gap between the lowest eigen- 
value, pS/V, and the second lowest and higher eigenvalues. Thus, the lowest 
eigenvalue is the dominant term, and it depends only on S/V: No other de- 
tail of the microstructure enters. This is because the fast diffusion equalizes 
the spin density everywhere within the pore space. The fine details of the 
pore geometry thus do not play a role in the magnetization decay, except at 
extremely short times. 
In the slow diffusion regime, the lowest eigenvalue is of the order of a2/D, 
and expectedly, somewhat different for different pore shapes. Its value is very 
close to the higher eigenvalues which contain detailed information on the 
pore geometry. In this case, the magnetization decay has a multiexponential 
behavior, even for a single pore. 
Figure 4.1 illustrates the fast and slow diffusion behavior in a simple 
pictorial way. On the left is the fast diffusion regime. Because of the fast dif- 
fusion and the small surface relaxivity, the unrelaxed spin density is uniform 
over the pore space. When these spins wander around and come into contact 
with the surface and relax, the unrelaxed spin population is quickly equalized 
through the diffusion process. As a result, the magnetization decays with a 
uniform spin density. On the right is the slow diffusion regime. In this case, 
the surface relaxivity is large and the diffusion is slow. The unrelaxed spin 
density near the surface decreases faster than the rate at which the unrelaxed 
spins can flow in, by diffusion from other parts of the pore space, to replace 
the loss. The spin density thus develops a profile which is high at the center 
of the pore and near zero at the pore surface.

becomes the basis for the assertion that NMR relaxation time distribution 
reflects the pore size distribution.

Surface relaxivity 
To treat the relaxation time distribution as a reflection of pore size distri- 
bution, not only do we have to be in the fast diffusion regime, but also the 
surface relaxivity, or the average surface density of paramagnetic impurity 
ions for each pore, should be nearly the same over the whole rock. 
Surprisingly, numerous experiments on brine-saturated rock samples 
(Kenyon et al. [117, 118, 119], Straley et al. [208], Loren and Robinson [146]) 
have shown that indeed the relaxation time distribution curve is very simi- 
lar to the pore size distribution curve obtained by other techniques such as 
mercury injection. Naturally, such similarity is not universal, as the NMR is 
a measure of the size of the pore bodies, whereas the mercury porosimetry is 
a measure of the size of the pore throats. However, if there is a reasonably 
consistent factor between the pore throat and the pore body, then the NMR 
relaxation time distribution can be quite similar to the pore size distribution 
determined by mercury porosimetry. Of course, if the average surface relax- 
ivity p is not fairly constant over the whole scale, then the similarity between 
the two distributions can be affected.

The T2 relaxation process in rocks is controlled by a number of mech- 
anisms. Bulk relaxation can often be neglected. The surface relaxation 
mechanism is the same as the one that dominates the T~ relaxation process 
in rocks, and provides the link to the pore size distribution of rocks. The 
third mechanism is molecular diffusion in macroscopic and microscopic field 
gradients. The CPMG pulse sequence and low Larmor frequencies can be 
used to minimize this diffusion effect. However, the internal gradients, in- 
duced by magnetic susceptibility contrasts between the pore fluid and solid 
matrix, occur at the pore scale and are difficult to eliminate completely by 
the CPMG pulse sequence. This kind of internal field gradient due to mag- 
netic susceptibility contrast is proportional to the applied field strength. The 
enhanced relaxation rate it brings about is proportional to the square of the 
applied field strength. It is the primary source for the difference between T~ 
and T2 distributions [19]. As the Larmor frequency increases, the T~/T2 ratio 
is expected to increase. 
Kleinberg et al.'s work [126, 127] has shown that aside from special cir- 
cumstances such as the existence of extremely large internal field gradients, 
T2 distribution can reflect the pore size distribution of rocks with limitations 
similar to those in the case of T~ distribution. Their work also provides the 
important information that the T1/T2 ratio at 2 MHz is roughly around 1.65. 
This information can be used for porosity corrections in data inversion pro- 
cess when the repeat delay time between excitations during logging is not 
sufficient to have a complete polarization recovery of the spins.

NMR behavior of different rock types 
The T1 or T2 distributions of sedimentary rocks have been studied exten- 
sively. In such rocks, consistent NMR behaviors have been observed and 
correlated to petrophysical parameters of interest to the petroleum industry. 
Laboratory measurements [127] have shown that sandstones typically have a 
paramagnetic ion content of 1% and grain susceptibilities ~ 10 -s cgs/cm 3. 
The surface relaxivity typically falls in a narrow range of 1-10 #m/s [125, 32]. 
The T2 distribution is often highly correlated to the pore size distribution. 
When the grains are well sorted, the T2 distribution can display a single 
peak at long relaxation times. Typically, a short relaxation time component 
is present as a shoulder or a separate bump indicating the presence of fine 
silt with capillary bound water or clays. The permeability formula devel- 
oped from laboratory measurements usually works quite well for predicting 
permeability from NMR logs. 
Carbonates 
Carbonates usually have a surface relaxivity almost an order of magnitude 
smaller than that of sandstones. As a result, the diffusion coupling between 
pores is stronger. Thus, different pore scales, which would have different re- 
laxation times in sandstones, are coupled to each other through the diffusion 
process, and merge to yield an intermediate state, or in the extreme case, a 
single relaxation time characteristic of the combined surface to volume ra- 
tio. Hence, the T2 distribution of a carbonate may not reflect the pore size 
distribution of the rock. 
Ramakrishnan et al. [183, 184], using a random walk simulation, were 
able to demonstrate that, indeed, the surface relaxivity plays an important 
role in determining whether the NMR relaxation time distribution reflects 
the pore size distribution. If the surface relaxivity is weak, spins diffuse easily 
between large and small pores, there can be a strong coupling of the decay of 
the magnetizations for large and small pores, and the NMR relaxation time 
distribution no longer reflects the pore size distribution.

three-dimensional array of spherical grains surrounded by intergranular pore

4.2.4 Internal gradients 
The internal field gradients of porous media are induced mainly by the mag- 
netic susceptibility contrast between the solid matrix and pore fluid. The 
magnitudes of these internal gradients depend not only on the magnetic sus- 
ceptibility contrast but also on the pore geometries. The local field gradient 
can be extremely high near the sharp corners of solid grains. When spins 
diffuse near those corners, they can be quickly relaxed. 
Strong internal field gradients present at least two problems. First, T2 of 
the short relaxation time components can relax so fast that the signal is not 
captured, leading to an underestimated porosity. Second, the shift of the T2 
peaks towards shorter relaxation times can cause errors in the computation 
of the diffusion coefficient or viscosity of the oils, or other errors in the in-

4.3 Petrophysical applications 
To understand and/or interpret the results of NMR logs from borehole mea- 
surements, it is imperative to conduct NMR laboratory measurements on 
brine and/or oil saturated rock samples first. Such measurements can be 
carried out to almost any desired accuracy using various means not readily 
available in a logging situation. For example, the measured NMR signals 
can be conveniently stacked many times to obtain a very high signal to noise 
ratio. One can study how the level of noise tends to affect the characteristics 
of the T2 distributions. When the water and oil signals are both present in 
the measurements, one can soak the core plugs in DeO to remove the water 
signal, or use the Magic Angle Spinning (MAS) technique to differentiate the 
two phases in high magnetic field. The determination of irreducible water 
saturation, the understanding of oil T2 relaxation times with respect to its 
viscosity and diffusion coefficient, as well as the establishment of permeability 
estimators, are all important steps in the laboratory for the understanding, 
interpretation, and applications of NMR logs to formation evaluation. In the 
following we shall discuss them in detail. 
4.3.1 Porosity 
The NMR signal, when extrapolated back to zero time (in a T~ or T2 mea- 
surement), is proportional to the population of the hydrogen nuclei in the 
probed zone. When properly calibrated, the NMR measurement is an ex- 
cellent way to measure porosity. Figure 4.11 (from Straley [208]) shows 
the excellent comparison of 2 MHz NMR porosity of T2 measurements with 
buoyancy porosity for 192 sandstones. However, if the T2 measurement is 
conducted at very high magnetic field, the correlation can be poor. This is 
because the magnetic susceptibility contrast induced internal field gradients 
are proportional to the applied field strength, and the enhanced relaxation

4.5 NMR core imaging 
NMR imaging is mainly used for core imaging in a similar way as the X-ray 
CAT (Computer-Aided Tomography) scans are used in core studies. The 
purpose is to understand the fluid distributions within a core before, during, 
and after a core-flood experiment. The X-ray CAT scan image is based on the 
contrast in X-ray absorption in different parts of the core. The images depend 
largely on the high X-ray absortion of the minerals in the solid matrix, not 
that of the pore fluids. In contrast, all the signal in NMR images comes from 
the hydrogen nuclei in water or oil in the pore space. The matrix does not 
contribute to the signal at all. The two techniques are thus complementary. 
Unlike the petrophysical applications of other NMR properties, which 
have a close relationship to rock properties such as irreducible water satura- 
tion, pore size distribution, and permeability, NMR core imaging is at present 
mostly limited to studies of fluid distributions within the pore space. In the 
future, NMR core imaging may very well develop into a tool for wettability 
studies and other applications. Because of its importance in core study, as 
well as in biology and medicine, we shall briefly outline its principles here. 
For a detailed account, the readers should refer to Callaghan [49].

In the above discussion of Eq. (4.52), we neglected the T2 spin relaxation. 
When this is not justified, special pulsing schemes should be employed in 
order to obtain a faithful NMR image. 
To illustrate how this is typically done in practice, we consider the exam- 
ple outlined in Figure 4.28: First, a 90 ~ rf pulse, with a sinc function (sin x/x) 
envelope and a mean carrier frequency ~0, generates a square shaped slice 
in the frequency domain while a large field gradient G~ is applied along z, in 
the same direction as the mean spin polarizing field Hoez. This procedure 
selects a thin slab or slice of the sample, parallel to the xy plane. The spins 
in this slice have a narrow range of Larmor precession frequencies centered 
around w0 and are tilted by the rf pulse into the xy plane. The thickness of 
this slice is determined by the length of the sinc function and the magnitude 
of G~. Only the spins in this thin slice are in resonance with the rf pulse and 
are, therefore, tipped to the xy plane. Some phase spreading of these spins 
will occur as a result of the large gradient G~. The field gradient is then 
turned off, after which a 180 ~ spin inverting rf pulse is applied. Then the 
same field gradient Gz is turned on again, but for a different length of time, 
in order to refocus the phases of the precessing spins in the selected slice in 
an optimal fashion. The purpose of the 180 ~ rf pulse and the subsequent 
pulse field gradient G~ is to make all the precessing spins in the selected slice 
have nearly the same phase and frequency, in preparation for the subsequent 
phase and frequency encoding. 
At this juncture, a pulse field gradient Gy is turned on, along y, for a 
time ty. This causes each spin to precess at a rate that depends on its y 
coordinate. Thus, it acquires a phase which depends on that coordinate. 
This corresponds to shifting the origin in k space by an amount ky = ~/Gyt~ 
along y. This is commonly referred to as "phase encoding". 
After that, the so-called "reac~' gradient Gx is turned on, along x, and 
the FID signal is collected. The gradient G~ now causes the spins with the 
same y coordinate, which used to have the same rate of precession, to be 
split into groups with different Larmor frequencies, varying in linear fashion 
with x. This part of the procedure is called "frequency encoding". 
While this gradient G~ is left on, the FID signal is collected from the 
entire system, as a function of the time t~ elapsed since that gradient was 
turned on. The signal comes from only those spins in the original xy slice 
that were tipped to the xy plane in the first place, all of which have the same 
value of z! Each digitized signal of the FID corresponds to a particular value 
of tx, and thus to a particular value of k~ = ~/G~tx. Therefore the signal,
