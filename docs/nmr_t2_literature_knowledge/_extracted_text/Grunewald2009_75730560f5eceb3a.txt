A laboratory study of NMR relaxation times and
pore coupling in heterogeneous media
Elliot Grunewald 1 and Rosemary Knight 1
ABSTRACT
Nuclear magnetic resonance /H20849NMR/H20850relaxation times of
geologic materials are closely related to pore geometry. In
heterogeneous media, however, the details of this relation-
ship are poorly understood because of a phenomenon known
as pore coupling, which arises when diffusing protons sample
multiple pores before relaxing. Laboratory experiments al-
low us to explore whether surface geochemistry can inﬂu-
ence pore coupling and how this process affects the observed
relaxation-time distribution. Measurements of the NMR re-
sponse for microporous silica gel packs, treated with varying
amounts of surface-coating iron, demonstrate that samples
with less iron exhibit stronger pore coupling than those with
abundant iron. When pore coupling is strong, the relaxation-
time distribution grossly misrepresents the underlying bimo-
dal pore-size distribution of micropores and macropores.
Speciﬁcally, the bimodal relaxation-time distribution be-
comes merged and the relative amplitude of the peaks fails to
reﬂect the true macropore and micropore volume. A reduc-
tion in pore coupling, observed with increasing iron content,
is attributed to a decrease in the distance protons are able to
diffuse before relaxing. Basic parameters describing the
shape of the relaxation-time distributions for this range of
samples are well-predicted by a 1D analytical model. Experi-
mental results conclusively demonstrate that surface geo-
chemistry is an important factor determining the degree to
which pore coupling occurs and illustrate how this phenome-
non can affect the interpretation of NMR relaxation measure-
ments in heterogeneous porous media.
INTRODUCTION
Nuclear magnetic resonance /H20849NMR/H20850relaxation measurements
provide an unparalleled view into the pore space of geologic materi-
als. The proton NMR method probes the time required for hydrogen
nuclei in the pore water to relax to equilibrium through diffusion and
interaction with the pore-scale physicochemical environment. NMR
relaxation-time measurements have been used for decades in geo-
physical well logging to estimate hydrocarbon-reservoir properties
such as porosity, pore-size distribution, and permeability /H20849Seevers,
1966; Kenyon et al., 1988/H20850. Recent research has extended NMR geo-
physical techniques to near-surface environmental applications and
has led to the development of a surface-based NMR method — mag-
netic resonance sounding /H20849MRS/H20850— to measure the NMR response
of groundwater aquifers /H20849Legchenko and V alla, 2002 ; Hertrich,
2008; Walsh, 2008/H20850. Our current study seeks to improve the interpre-
tation of near-surface NMR measurements by exploring the link be-
tween NMR relaxation times and pore-scale properties in heteroge-
neous porous media.
The analysis of NMR relaxation measurements in geologic mate-
rials requires an accurate model of the physical processes governing
relaxation. Conventional interpretation of these data assumes a mod-
el of isolated pores in which each diffusing proton samples only one
pore. Thus, it is assumed that each pore contributes independently to
the NMR signal, with a characteristic relaxation time. In many near-
surface materials, however, pores are sufﬁciently connected that dif-
fusing protons can readily sample multiple pores during NMR mea-
surement. For materials in which pores are strongly coupled by dif-
fusion, the resulting relaxation-time distribution reﬂects a complex
averaging of the pore network that can be difﬁcult to interpret.
Understanding the conditions in which pore coupling occurs and
how this process can inﬂuence NMR relaxation measurement is fun-
damental to the successful use of NMR data in geophysical applica-
tions. Several studies have explored this issue theoretically, using
models of idealized pore networks to determine how factors such as
pore geometry /H20849Cohen and Mendelson, 1982 ; McCall et al., 1991 ;
Zielinski et al., 2002 /H20850and surface geochemistry /H20849Ramakrishnan et
al., 1999; Ramakrishnan et al., 2001/H20850might control the extent of pore
coupling. Despite the clear need to account for pore-coupling ef-
fects, relatively few laboratory studies have explored this phenome-
non. Toumelin et al./H208492002/H20850show that interpore diffusion can strong-

METHODS AND MATERIALS
Sample characterization and preparation
The four samples used in this study are derived from a high-purity
silica gel /H20849Davisil® 150 Å 60–200 mesh, manufactured by Grace
Davison/H20850that has a bimodal pore-size distribution comprised of in-
tragranular micropores /H20849within the grains /H20850, reported to be approxi-
mately 15 nm in diameter, and larger intergranular macropores /H20849be-
tween the grains/H20850. We ﬁrst sieved the pure gel to achieve a narrower
grain-size distribution of 53– 105 /H9262m. The diameter of intragranu-
lar pores in a random packing is estimated to be about 20 /H9262m, as-
suming a ratio of average pore size to grain size of 0.225/H20849Barker and
Mehta, 1992/H20850. The micropore volume, expressed as milliliters/gram
gel, was provided from manufacturer speciﬁcations as 1.2 ml/ g. W e
measured the surface area of the gels /H20849315.1/H115062.5 m 2 / g/H20850by the
Brunauer-Emmett-Teller/H20849BET/H20850nitrogen adsorption method using a
Micromeritics ASAP 2020 accelerated surface area and porosimetry
system.
Our experimental approach was based on the assumption that in-
creasing the amount of paramagnetic Fe
3/H11501on the gel surface would
reduce pore coupling by increasing the surface relaxation rate; there-
fore, we closely controlled surface geochemistry. We rinsed the gel
repeatedly with 10% hydrogen chloride /H20849HCl/H20850and distilled, deion-
ized /H20849DDI/H20850water /H2084918 M /H9024cm/H20850to remove paramagnetic impurities,
and we retained a portion of the cleaned gel to be used as the highest
purity sample for NMR analysis. We then synthesized three addi-
tional gels with varied amounts of paramagnetic Fe
3/H11501oxyhydroxide
surface coating from the cleaned gel using methods of Grantham et
al. /H208491997/H20850. Samples were placed under vacuum and saturated with
aqueous solutions of ferrous sulfate /H20849FeSO4/H20850. The mixtures were
then oxidized by adding excess 30% hydrogen peroxide /H20849H2O2/H20850and
were placed on an orbital shaker to equilibrate overnight. Following
the iron coating treatment, the gels were rinsed with DDI water and
oven dried overnight at 45° C.
We determined the amount of paramagnetic Fe
3/H11501sorbed to each
gel from subsamples by atomic adsorption spectroscopy; concentra-
tions of Fe
3/H11501by mass in the four samples ranged from 0.00% in the
pure sample to 0.10% in the most heavily coated sample. Qualitative
analysis by scanning electron microscopy/H20849SEM/H20850and energy disper-
sion spectrometry /H20849EDS/H20850conﬁrmed that iron was effectively sorbed
to the interior and exterior surfaces of the gels. Neither the porosity
nor the surface area of the gels was changed, within experimental er-
ror, by the iron coating process.
NMR measurements
For NMR measurements, we packed dry samples of each gel in
consistent weights and volumes into cylindrical Teﬂon™ sample
holders with an interior diameter of 2.1 cm and height of 6 cm. In the
ﬁrst series of measurements, samples were completely saturated
with DDI water. To ensure maximum saturation, we ﬁrst evacuated
each packed sample in a dry vacuum /H2084975 mm mercury /H20850for 30 min
and then submerged the sample in a water bath before atmospheric
pressure was restored. Samples were allowed to saturate for 2 hr
while submerged. The volume of water held in micropores for each
sample was calculated from the mass of dry sample/H20849using manufac-
turer-speciﬁed 1.2 ml / g gel /H20850; the volume of water in macropores
was determined gravimetrically as the difference between total wa-
ter volume and micropore volume. We determined that of the total
water volume, 58% was held in micropores and 42% was held in
macropores.
We acquired a ﬁrst series of NMR measurements directly after
saturation. Following measurement at saturated conditions, we cen-
trifuged the packed samples to drain water from the large intergranu-
lar pores. Centrifuging removed approximately 98% of the inter-
granular pore water /H20849estimated gravimetrically /H20850; the intragranular
porosity was left undrained by this process as a result of the extreme-
ly high capillary potential of the micropores. We then acquired a sec-
ond series of NMR measurements, immediately after centrifuging,
at which point the NMR signal was presumed to be primarily sensi-
tive to water in the undrained intragranular micropores.
All NMR relaxation data were collected using a 2.2-MHz Maran
Ultra NMR core analyzer /H20849Resonance Instruments /H20850with a CPMG
pulse sequence. This instrument has a dead time of approximately
60 /H9262s. Data were acquired consistently at 30° C and were stacked
100 times to improve the signal-to-noise ratio /H20849S/N/H20850. Measurements
at varied echo-time t
E spacing indicated that magnetic gradient ef-
fects were negligible; therefore, the shortest available spacing, tE
/H11505150 /H9262s, was used to maximize data sampling at short times. Inde-
pendent measurements on each sample were repeated three times to
determine experimental variance.
We inverted each measured magnetization decay signal for a T
2
relaxation-time distribution using a regularized nonnegative least-
squares algorithm developed after Whittall et al. /H208491991/H20850. In this ap-
proach, the data are ﬁt to 200 logarithmically spacedT2 values, from
1 ms to 10 s, and the inverted model is regularized for smoothness.
Regularization parameters were selected such that the data were ﬁt
to a tolerance equal to the measured noise level. This statistically ro-
bust approach limits inversion artifacts and is required in order to as-

CONCLUSIONS
The NMR relaxation times of porous geologic materials are close-
ly linked to the volume and geometry of the water-saturated pore
space. Thus, there is tremendous potential for using NMR relaxation
measurements, acquired with borehole or surface-based instru-
ments, in near-surface applications to estimate subsurface proper-
ties. It is clear, however, that substantial research is needed to fully
understand the processes governing NMR relaxation in near-surface
materials. This study has focused on the impact of pore coupling, a
process that can signiﬁcantly affect NMR relaxation measurements
in systems with well-connected pores.
Our experimental results demonstrate conclusively that surface
geochemistry can be a signiﬁcant factor determining the strength of
pore coupling and thus the relationship between pore geometry and
NMR relaxation times. By controlling for surface geochemistry in
well-characterized samples, we directly assessed the impact of pore
coupling on the observed T
2 distribution in samples representing a
range of coupling strengths. For materials with intragranular mi-
croporosity and intergranular macroporosity, pore coupling reduces
the slowest relaxation time associated with macropores and decreas-
es the amplitude of the fast relaxing signal associated with the mi-
cropores. If unaccounted for in the interpretation of the NMR relax-
ation data, the presence of pore coupling would lead to an underesti-
mation of the total micropore volume and the size of the largest
pores. Good agreement between our laboratory results and an ana-
lytical model suggests that a theoretical approach may help predict
the extent to which pore coupling will affect NMR measurements in
other porous media.
Our study has only considered the behavior of silica gels, which
have smaller pores and a lower
/H92672 value than is typical for most geo-
logic materials. The small pores tend to limit the extent of the diffu-
sion, whereas the low
/H92672 value tends to enhance the extent of diffu-
sion. Although these properties of silica gel are different from those
of a typical geologic material, the well-characterized geometry of
the gel allows us to investigate the physics linking surface chemistry
and pore coupling. Our ﬁndings lead us to anticipate that for a wide
class of unconsolidated materials with well-connected pores, the
NMR relaxation-time distribution will not accurately convey the un-
derlying pore structure because of pore coupling effects. Additional
research is required to explore the impact of pore coupling in a
broader range of materials to improve the interpretation of NMR
data and to advance the use of NMR methods in near-surface appli-
cations.
