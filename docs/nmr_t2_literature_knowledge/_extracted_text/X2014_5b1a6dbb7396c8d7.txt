Analytic theory of two-dimensional NMR in systems with coupled macro- and micropores
David Linton Johnson* and Lawrence M. Schwartz
Schlumberger Doll Research, One Hampshire Street, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02139, USA
(Received 17 December 2013; published 29 September 2014)
Two-dimensional (2D) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments involve a sequence of longitudinal
(T1) and transverse ( T2) measurements. When such experiments are applied to porous media, they are believed
to provide new and important information regarding diffusive coupling between distinct pore subpopulations.
However, we show in this paper that, in many cases of interest, this is simply not true. One often encounters
systems in which the one-dimensional (1D) T
1 and T2 processes are each controlled by just two distinct decay
modes. If these modes form a complete set, then one can derive analytic formulas that describe, exactly, the 2D
NMR measurements. Therefore, for such systems, the 2D measurements bring no additional information over
that which is already present in the 1D results. Our predictions agree quite well with numerical results based on
the microporous grain-consolidation (μ − GC) model.

I. INTRODUCTION
Two-dimensional (2D) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
experiments have recently been applied to a wide range of ﬂuid
ﬁlled porous media including reservoir rocks [ 1–4], cements
[5], catalysts [ 6], and surfactants [ 7]. These measurements
provide a correlation between the initial (encoding) and ﬁnal
(detection) part of the experiment. Of particular interest is the
T
2-T1-T2 (also known as T2-storage-T2) protocol in which the
spins are ﬁrst rotated to be perpendicular to the static magnetic
ﬁeld, B
0, where they undergo transverse ( T2) relaxation for
a variable amount of time, τ1. Next, they are rotated to be
parallel to B0 where they undergo longitudinal (T1) relaxation
for a ﬁxed amount of storage time, ts . Finally, the spins are
rotated back to the transverse plane and undergo transverse
relaxation for a second variable amount of time, τ
2 [3,5,8].
Typically for each value of the storage time, ts , the NMR data
are processed to produce lifetime distributions P(ts ; T a
2 ,T b
2 )
where T a(b)
2 refers to a decay time relevant to the ﬁrst (second)
T2 part of the experiment.
A T2-storage-T2 example, based on microporous grain-
consolidation ( μ − GC) simulations [ 8] described below, is
shown in Fig. 1 together with the corresponding 1D T2
distribution. In the 2D cross-plot off-diagonal peaks are clearly
evident. These peaks have been interpreted as providing new
information regarding diffusive coupling between distinct spin
populations (e.g., micro- and macropores in the μ − GC
model) [ 8]. Indeed, by monitoring the amplitude of these
off-diagonal peaks as a function of the storage time, t
s,
several authors have estimated exchange rates or coupling
parameters [3–5]. While this approach is intuitively appealing,
we will show that the behavior of the off-diagonal peaks
can sometimes be predicted from the one-dimensional (1D)
lifetime distributions. In such cases no new information is
contained in the T
2-T2 cross-plot. Thus, we maintain, the very
signiﬁcant complexity of doing the 2D experiments as opposed
to the more conventional 1D experiments (CPMG for T
2 plus
inversion recovery for T1 [1–7]) is often unwarranted.

We consider here the simple but important examples of 2D
NMR, namely those for which the relevant 1D relaxation can
be described as the sum of two exponential functions. (This
includes, as a subset, the two-site exchange model which has
often been used to interpret 2D NMR data [ 5,8].) The analytic
theory we develop allows us to predict, with no additional
information, the integrated intensity of each of the peaks
appearing in two-dimensional cross-plots of the kind shown in
Fig. 1. Therefore, for such systems, the 2D measurement brings
no additional information over that which is already present
in the 1D results. Equally important, our analysis points the
way forward if we are to understand the value of 2D NMR in
more complicated systems. We present numerical results on a
system in which three modes are needed to describe the 1D
lifetime distributions. Our analysis can be extended to such
systems and should allow us to identify which features of the
2D distributions contain information that is not already present
in the 1D spectra.

using the parameters ρ2 = 2 μm/s and ρ1 = 0, 0.25, 0.5, and
1.0 μm/s. A large number of random walkers (typically 10 6)
are launched randomly and uniformly within the pore space.
For the initial part of the simulation, t ⩽ τ
1, relaxation at the
pore-grain interface is controlled by ρ2. At the ﬁrst switching
time, τ1, the surviving walkers continue but with a surface
relaxation rate, ρ1. Then at the second switching time, τ1 + ts ,
the surface relaxivity reverts to ρ2. We processed the time
domain data using an inverse 2D Fredholm integral equation
solver to generate two dimensional T2-T2 contour plots, one of
which is shown in Fig. 1. We integrated the volume under each
peak in order to determine Mij for each value of the storage
time, ts . The results are shown in Fig. 6 along with the analytic
results of Eqs. ( 12) using as input data the parameters listed
in Table I. We see that the theory works quite well for this
geometry and for these choices of relaxivity values [ 14]. We
also see that the simulation data obeys the sum rule Eq. ( 8).
In principle, this comparison could have been made based
on just the time domain data using the deﬁnition, Eq. ( 6) and
the model, Eqs. (12). This would have involved the analysis of
a large number of 1D data sets (one for each value of τ1 and
ts ). We employed the 2D Fredholm equation solver as a matter
of convenience; its use is not essential.
Obviously, there are many conditions under which it will
not be possible to express both of the 1D decays as the sum of
two exponentials. In such cases we want to understand what
features of the 2D NMR spectrum contain information that
cannot be derived from the 1D distributions. An example can
be seen in the μ − GC model if we enlarge the unit cell size
by a factor of two and increase the values of ρ
1 and ρ2 as
shown in Fig. 7. In this case the 1D decays can be written as
the sum of three exponentials and it is possible to extend the
present theory [15]. In more complicated systems, our analysis
will inevitably breakdown and we will be in a position to
identify those features of the 2D spectra that truly contain new
information about diffusive coupling.
III. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, if the 1D decay functions,M1(t) andM2(t), can
be represented accurately as the sum of two exponentials, we
have developed an analytic theory for the results of T
1-T2 and
T2-storage-T2 experiments. The only inputs to the theory are
the decay rates and amplitudes of the two 1D measurements.
The validity of the theory has been conﬁrmed by comparison
against numerical modeling in a mixed pore size system. While
the present analysis is based on the diffusion equation and ﬂuid
ﬁlled porous media, our results apply equally to measurements
on surfactants and catalysts where the evolution of the
magnetization is governed by a different self-adjoint operator.
The only other requirement is that the initial magnetization
and the ﬁnal amplitude measurement are described by the
same function, m
0.
