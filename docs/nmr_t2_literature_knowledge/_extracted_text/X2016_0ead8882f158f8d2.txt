abstract
Two-dimensional spin–spin relaxation ( T2–T2) techniques have been developed to probe coupling
between different environments such as diffusive coupling between small and large pores or chemical
exchange with clays. In these studies, Numerical Laplace Inversion (NLI) is used to obtain two-
dimensional T
2–T2 relaxation distribution spectrum from the T2–T2 signal decays, and the off-diagonal
peaks characterize coupling. Often, these coupling peaks are both weak and close to the diagonal and
combined with the inherently ill-conditioned nature of the inversion, their presence is difﬁcult to differ-
entiate from inversion related artifacts and blurring. This manuscript presents a time domain based anal-
ysis to identify the presence of coupling that avoids the ambiguities present in T
2–T2 spectra. The
approach utilizes the symmetric nature of the two-dimensional time domain data, where the presence
of curvature along one of these symmetries gives an unambiguous indicator of coupling.
Measurements on porous glass beads are used to verify the technique.

1. Introduction
NMR relaxometry yields information about diffusion and
molecular dynamics [1–3], and requires minimal ﬁeld homogene-
ity unlike conventional NMR spectroscopy and imaging techniques
[4]. Relaxometry thus enables a wide range of applications for NMR
in small and portable devices. Characterization of NMR T2 relax-
ation is a common core component in many applications due to
both its natural measurement in any refocusing pulse sequence
and broad utility in porous media analysis [5–13]. T2 measure-
ments are often used to analyze a wide variety of biological [14],
geological [15] and synthetic materials [16–18] to estimate quan-
tities such as the pore size distribution and ﬂuid composition
(e.g. oil and water content) [3,8,9,19,20]. T2 relaxation measure-
ments furthermore obtain these quantities in a safe and non-
intrusive way [4] both in and outside of the laboratory. For
example, a major commercial application of NMR relaxation
measurements is to assess the pore size distributions and ﬂuid
content of subsurface rock formation in oil well logging [21,22].
Although one-dimensional T1 and T2 relaxation distributions
can provide the physical properties of porous media, just as in
one-dimensional NMR spectroscopy, they provide limited informa-
tion to resolve multiple components and correlations between
different NMR properties. Multidimensional relaxometry tech-
niques have often proven to be able to address these limitations.
Techniques like diffusion– T2 (D–T2) [23–25], diffusion–diffusion
(D–D) [26,27] and T1–T2 [28,29] correlation techniques have been
developed to better analyze pore geometry [27,30,31], identify
exchange [1,12,32], and improve ﬂuid identiﬁcation [20,33,34].
Similar to these multi-dimensional low-ﬁeld NMR techniques, T2
can also be correlated to itself. The ( T2–T2) relaxation exchange
measurement not only includes the information that 1D T2 mea-
surement offers, but also gives addition information on molecular
dynamics, connectivity, as well as T1 information [21,35]. It has
been used to measure the convective ﬂow in partially saturated
soil samples [4], violations of the fast diffusion limit [37] and to
study diffusional coupling between different pores or in chemical
exchange [12,15,35].
For porous media, 2 the exchange peaks in the T2–T2 spectra can
be roughly thought of as the spins that diffuse between different
pores. Technically, these peaks correspond to exchange between T2
relaxation eigenmodes (which are not necessarily exactly the same
as pores) and their mutual overlap with the T1 relaxation eigen-
modes [35,36]. This places further requirements on the relaxation
properties to observe exchange, such as differing T1 and T2 surface
relativities and the requirement that one of the pores is not in the
fast diffusion limit [35]. Nonetheless, the exchange information
T2–T2 contains is not easily available from other multidimensional
NMR relaxometry techniques. For instance, while T1–T2 experiments

T2–T2 measurements of porous media will often detect signals
consisting of multiple T2 decays that correspond to different sam-
ple components. Numeric Laplace Inversion (NLI) is necessary to
convert this multi-exponential two-dimensional decay data into
a T2–T2 spectrum. Then, a sample with a single T2 relaxation com-
ponent such as pure water would yield the same exponential decay
along each of the two measurement dimensions and would corre-
spond to a single peak along the diagonal of the T2–T2 spectrum.
Similarly, a sample with two distinct T2 relaxation components
should result in a T2–T2 spectrum with two separate peaks on
the main diagonal line and a pair of off-diagonal peaks arising from
exchange between these two components as illustrated in Fig. 1 .
Saturated porous media typically have even more complex spectra.
In natural samples, for instance, soil [21] or rocks, the distributions
of pore sizes is rarely mono-disperse and instead form an essen-
tially continuous distribution of pore sizes. Then, the correspond-
ing diagonal T2–T2 peaks will often overlap with each other and
form a long continuous peak that reﬂects the pore size distribution.
Furthermore, even in two component systems, the corresponding
two diagonal peaks can easily blur together due to inherently lim-
ited resolution of NLI. All NLI algorithms must solve an ill-
conditioned inversion, which means that a wide range of solutions
exist that will ﬁt the data within the noise. While there are a num-
ber of regularization techniques [38–41] that can give stable and
reasonable spectra, the ill-posed nature of the inversion intrinsi-
cally limits the resolution of different T2 values and large differ-
ences in signal strength, and makes the inversion’s performance
signal to noise ratio (SNR) dependent.
T2–T2 applications in particular frequently push the resolution
limits of NLI, where exchange peaks are often weak and adjacent
to intense diagonal peaks. Then the optimization of the reconstruc-
tion faces the compromise where use of a conservative regulariza-
tion parameter (allowing for a larger misﬁt of the spectra to the
acquired data) broadens the diagonal-peak to the point of obscur-
ing the presence of cross-peaks, while weakening regularization
risks the introduction of artifact peaks that may be mistaken for
legitimate cross peaks as the inversion is forced to ﬁt noise in
the spectrum [42]. This compromise often makes it difﬁcult to
resolve the presence of coupling in T2–T2 correlation maps without
unambiguity and worsens with low SNR data [43]. This limitation
is intrinsic to T2–T2 correlation maps, limiting the ability of the
technique to characterize exchange in cases of strong coupling
between distinct T2 components. In this paper, we introduce a
T2–T2 analysis that can unambiguously identify the presence of
exchange by directly analyzing the time domain data. In this
way, the limitations of NLI that obscure the presence of cross-
peaks are avoided to enable the robust identiﬁcation of exchange.
2. Theory
The Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) is a common pulse
sequence [44–46] to measure T
2 relaxation (see Fig. 2a), and forms
the building block for the T2–T2 experiment. The CPMG sequence
includes a series of refocusing pulse to minimize the additional sig-
nal decay due magnetic ﬁeld inhomogeneity. It starts with a nom-
inal 90/C176pulse to excite the magnetization into the transverse plane
and is followed by a train of 180 /C176pulses to repeatedly re-focus the
signal. The spacing time between adjacent 180 /C176pulse is called echo
time, te, and corresponds to the spacing between the refocused sig-
nal. The timing between the initial 90 /C176and 180 /C176pulses is half the
echo time and can be improved by shortening the delay to com-
pensate for spin precession during the excitation pulse [47].
The T2–T2 pulse sequence consists of three parts shown in
Fig. 2 b and essentially consists of two CPMG sequences joined by
a mixing period. During the ﬁrst encoding period s1, any given spin
population in a particular chemical environment will have its sig-
nal decay as in a 1D CPMG experiment, and its T2 labels the initial
spin population. Typically, no signal is acquired during this period.
The second section is the mixing period, and consists of two 90 /C176
pulses to form a stimulated echo over the mixing time ( sm). During
the mixing period, the spin populations undergo T1 relaxation in
order to preserve the magnetization and to allow for mixing
between different environments ( T2 components). For example,
the spins that belong to a spin population A with a relaxation time
T2a during s1 can diffuse into spin population B where its trans-
verse relaxation time would be T2b. The ﬁnal spin populations are
measured during the third section of the sequence, the detection
period (
s2), as in a standard CPMG acquisition. To form a two-
dimensional experiment, for each measurement the mixing time
and acquisition times s2 are ﬁxed, while only the value of s1 is
increased by varying the number of echoes during the ﬁrst period.
This time domain data is collected in a two-dimensional matrix
and an example of simulated T2–T2 data is shown in Fig. 2 d.
Assuming a static sample at equilibrium, the signal with respect
to these T2 periods can be shown to be symmetric. Following, the
derivation of the T2–T2 signal in [48] based on the Torrey–Bloch
equation, the dynamics of the magnetization during each of the
periods of the experiment when undergoing either T1 and T2 relax-
ation can be broken up into the sum of orthogonal relaxation
modes /n(r) of magnetization, each with some characteristic decay
time Tn.

3. Methods
Both simulation data and experimental data are used to verify
the signal symmetry and time domain analysis. All the simulation
data are calculated using Matlab /C211with two-site exchange kinetic
model [43]. The two site kinetic model, instead of the full model
[48], is selected for the simpliﬁed calculation of idealized T2–T2
data. Our use of the kinetic model is limited to two sites because
its implementation in [43] allows for asymmetry given greater
than two sites. This asymmetry is possible in their model for three
or more sites because it only assumes detailed mass balance. This
looser prior allows for non-equilibria phenomena such as convec-
tion and ﬂow that is not typical when analyzing most static porous
media samples.

4. Results and discussion
A set of T2–T2 measurements are performed on porous glass
beads, where we expect to observe exchange between the inter-
and intra-bead pores. In Fig. 4, the time domain data, T2–T2 spectra
and their sd slices are compared as the mixing time is changed over
0, 5, 50, and 100 ms. In the T2–T2 spectrum (Fig. 4a), a narrow diag-
onal line indicates that there is no exchange and is consistent with
the fact that a zero mixing time will not have time for exchange to
occur. As the mixing time is increased the diagonal peak broadens,
this is consistent with increasing exchange but it is also consistent
with resolution loss due to the lower SNR at longer mixing times
due to T1 relaxation and the SNR resolution dependence of NLI.
In the two-dimensional time domain data ( Fig. 4b), the red contour
lines also evolve with mixing time, starting with no variation along
sd, then increasing in curvature with longer mixing times. The evo-
lution of the curvature in time domain data is clearly observed for
the sd slice at ss = 85.04 ms as shown in Fig. 4 c. At zero mixing
time, where exchange is impossible, the sd line is essentially ﬂat,
and at the 100 ms mixing time, the sd line curves indicating the
presence of exchange.
The advantage of the time domain analysis ( Fig. 4b and c) is that
the indicator for exchange, curvature, cannot arise from noise. In
contrast, the indication of exchange from broadening in the T2–T2
spectra ( Fig. 4 a) can also occur due to greater regularization in
the inversion to compensate for worsening SNR at longer mixing
times.
A second feature of the T2–T2 signal decay (Eq. (8)) is that, in the
absence of exchange, the decay signal should purely be a function
of ss, independent of sd, and should match the ordinary CPMG
decay. A comparison between the ﬁrst CPMG curve in T2–T2 time
domain data ( s2 curve with s1 = 0.167 ms) and the decay along ss
diagonal ( sd = 0) are plotted in Fig. 5 b. In Fig. 5 a, the blue and the
red lines, respectively illustrate the locations of these decay curves
along s2 and ss. Along the s2 curve ss /C25 s2 since s1 is small
(0.167 ms or one echo). As predicted from the model (Eq. (8)),
the decay curves show a very good agreement between CPMG
decay and s2 slice plots (differing by less than the single echo noise
amplitude) in the absence of exchange at zero mixing time.
Finally, to conﬁrm that the curvature is indeed due to mixing
between T2 modes and not due to sequence artifacts at non-zero
mixing times, the measurements are repeated for two control sam-
ples where mixing is impossible. In one control experiment, the
sample consists of two separate bottles of NiCl doped water and
so exchange between them is impossible. In the second, the sample
consists of a non-porous mono-disperse glass-bead pack. Because
it has only a single pore size, due to random packing of mono-
disperse glass-beads, there are no other T2 components to
exchange with. In Fig. 6 , the sd cross-sections of the time domain
data are plotted and compared to the porous glass beads sample.
Consistent with the absence of exchange, there is no signiﬁcant
curvature observed for either sample.
In all of these experiments, the ss–sd time domain analysis pro-
vides a clear, distinct indicator to the presence of exchange peaks
in T2–T2 data, and so provides a valuable complement to the anal-
ysis of the T2–T2 relaxation spectra. This indicator appears regard-
less of the number of exchange peaks, but is only sensitive to their
presence. By directly analyzing the time domain data, one avoids
issues such as peak broadening and artifact peaks from NLI that
can easily interfere with the identiﬁcation of exchange peaks.
Thus, the time domain analysis can act as a valuable tool to iden-
tify the presence of exchange and guide the subsequent interpre-
tation of the T
2–T2 spectra. Application of the time-domain
analysis however must also consider that, although the experi-
mental data here agree with the modiﬁed kernel function (Eq.
(8)), the method relies on the T2–T2 signal having diagonal mirror
symmetry and this property presumes a system in equilibrium.
Otherwise, signal asymmetry may be possible as shown in recent
work that assumes a weaker prior, mass-balance [43]. For charac-
terizing most static samples, equilibrium is nonetheless a fair
assumption and can be ensured by appropriate experimental
design. The time-domain analysis may only not be potentially
applicable in measurements dynamic phenomena such as ﬂow
and monitoring chemical reactions, and deviations in T2–T2 signal
symmetry can then be used as an indicator for non-equilibrium
conditions.
Finally, as presented we only use a simple visual analysis to
determine the presence of curvature and identity exchange. Quan-

5. Conclusion
T2–T2 experiments have the valuable capacity to infer exchange,
providing information on molecular dynamics, connectivity and
diffusional coupling between different pores. However, its tradi-
tional analysis requires that the T2–T2 signal decay to ﬁrst be con-
verted into a two-dimensional T2–T2 relaxation spectrum, an
inherently ill-conditioned operation that can frequently hinder
the unambiguous identiﬁcation of exchange peaks. Instead, a direct
analysis of the time domain signal can avoid inversion so to
directly identify the presence of exchange. Curvature in the signal,
when plotted as a function of the difference of the observation and
encoding CPMG times ( sd = s2 /C0 s1), provides an unambiguous
indicator of exchange independent of the number of exchanging
T2 populations. This analysis also has the potential to the improve
quantiﬁcation of exchange by directly ﬁtting for off-diagonal peak
area and position. The method, when applied to porous glass-bead
samples, yields a clear indicator of the presence exchange even
when the corresponding T2–T2 spectral interpretations are ambigu-
ous and yield only a moderate widening of the on-diagonal non-
exchange peak that could also be due variations in the reconstruc-
tion parameters. Thus, we expect this time domain analysis to a
valuable tool to inform the T2–T2 spectral analysis, and improve
the T2–T2 measurement’s sensitivity to exchange.
