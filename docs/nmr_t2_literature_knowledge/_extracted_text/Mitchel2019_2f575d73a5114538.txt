ABSTRACT
Porous media characterized by a hierarchy of length scales are ubiquitous in industry and nature, and include carbonate rocks, cements,
heterogeneous catalysts, and biological cells. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a popular tool for studying liquid-saturated porous mate-
rials, where the spin relaxation rate is generally considered proportional to pore size. However, in porous granular media, the relaxation rate
is modiﬁed by diffusion between the intraparticle and interparticle pores. The observed relaxation rates do not reﬂect the pore size under
such conditions. Deconvolving the various contributions of surface relaxation, geometry, and diffusion is nontrivial, and forward models
are a powerful technique for elucidating the underlying pore structure. Various forward models have been proposed previously, including
analytic solutions and random walk simulations. Here, a ﬁnite element method is adopted to simulate the diffusion of nuclear magnetization
in a coupled pore geometry. We validate our model against existing solutions and use the simulations to determine the surface relaxivity of
powdered silica by matching experimental results. The ﬁnite element approach is more versatile than other modeling methods, allowing direct
visualization of the diffusing magnetization and being trivially extensible to multidimensional NMR exchange experiments.

I. INTRODUCTION
Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxation time measure-
ments are commonly used to probe the structure of liquid-saturated
porous media. Information on the conﬁning geometric length scales
is recovered by assuming that the observed relaxation rate (inverse
of time) is proportional to the surface-to-volume ratio S/V of
the pores. In complex materials, characterized by a hierarchy of
length scales, distributions of longitudinal T1 or transverse T2 relax-
ation time are used as a proxy for pore size distributions. 1 This
interpretation is based on the assumption that each pore is iso-
lated from its neighbors. 2 However, if pores of substantially dif-
ferent sizes are strongly coupled, this interpretation is no longer
valid.3
Materials characterized by coupled pore systems include lime-
stone,3–5 heterogeneous catalysts, 6,7 and cements. 8–10 We refer
to these materials as porous granular media as they comprise
connected “micropores” inside grains separated by “macropores”
(interparticle voids, vugs, fractures, or cracks). NMR has been
applied ubiquitously to the study of these heterogeneous materials
despite the persistent challenge of deconvolving the contributions
of surface relaxation and diffusion between the micropores
and macropores. Multidimensional NMR relaxation time corre-
lations, such as T1-T2
11 and T2-T2,12 are useful to elucidate the
structure-transport relations in analogy to chemical exchange spec-
troscopy.13 While these experiments highlight the presence of
exchange14,15 and can provide estimates of diffusional coupling con-
stants,5,9,16,17 the challenge of extracting geometric length scales
persists.
Forward modeling has been used to elucidate the underly-
ing pore structure. A model based on prior knowledge of the
sample (porosity, grain size, liquid diffusion coefﬁcient) is tested
against experimental NMR data to estimate the unknown param-
eters (pore size, surface relaxivity, diffusional coupling constant).

Several techniques have been applied to this problem. Analytic solu-
tions range from simple two-site exchange models 9,17,18 to a one-
dimensional (1D) porous grain. 3,4,19 Random walk simulations in
a three-dimensional (3D) periodic lattice (microporous grain con-
solidation model, µGC) have been used extensively to support the
interpretation of NMR relaxation time measurements. 3,20–23 Both
the analytic and random walk approaches have limitations. The ana-
lytic two-site exchange model is used to describe multidimensional
NMR experiments9,17,22,24 but assumes spatially uniform magnetiza-
tion. The analytic 1D porous grain model has not been extended
to represent multidimensional NMR experiments, and an approx-
imate extension to three spatial dimensions (based on the µGC
lattice) assumed uniform magnetization in the macropores. 3 The
random walk simulations are computationally expensive, and even
though each walker is independent giving rise to readily paral-
lelable code, simulations of a T2-T2 correlation can run to days
on a desktop personal computer (PC). 21 Furthermore, our avail-
able random walk simulation code is based on a primitive cubic
lattice of spherical solids and cannot readily accommodate arbi-
trary grain or pore geometries. However, more sophisticated ran-
dom walk methods have been presented in the literature based on
X-ray microcomputed tomography ( µ-CT) images with embedded
microporosity.25,26
Here, we adopt a ﬁnite element method to model the diffu-
sion of magnetization in a coupled pore system. For convenience,
we employ the commercial COMSOL Multiphysics® package. We
have previously used this software to explore T2 relaxation in the
presence of pore-scale magnetic ﬁeld inhomogeneities 27 and simu-
late two-site exchange.28 To simulate the diffusion of magnetization
in a granular porous medium, the model geometry is divided into
two continuum domains: the intergrain macropore and the micro-
porous grain.19 Due to the orders of magnitude difference in length
scale between the individual micropores and the macropore, it is not
practical to generate and mesh the exact geometry. Therefore, the
interior of the grain is represented as a spatially uniform domain
with volume-averaged properties (grain porosity µ, apparent diffu-
sion coefﬁcient Dapp, and relaxation time Tµ); these spatially aver-
aged properties are calculated in a separate model representing a
single micropore. Coupling between the microporous grain and the
macropore is established by way of matched boundary conditions on
either side of a smooth interface. All outer boundaries are reﬂecting
to represent a periodic geometry.
The general theory describing the diffusion of continuum
nuclear magnetization in a porous granular medium is presented
in Sec. II. An example periodic geometry is illustrated in Sec. III
based on the µGC model of Schwartz, 29 and we introduce the var-
ious simulations based on this geometry. The numerical methods
used to estimate relaxation time distributions from the magneti-
zation decay data are outlined in Sec. IV. The results obtained by
ﬁnite element analysis of a 1D geometry and analytic solutions
are compared in Sec. V A, and results from ﬁnite element anal-
ysis of a 3D µGC geometry are compared to random walk simu-
lations in Sec. V B. These comparisons serve to validate the use
of COMSOL Multiphysics for simulations of magnetization diffus-
ing in a coupled porous medium. We then extend the ﬁnite ele-
ment method to multidimensional NMR experiments and generate
T1-T2 correlations from the µGC geometry. Finally, we compare
experimental T1-T2 correlations to the simulations performed under
matching conditions in Sec. VII and show that the salient fea-
tures of the experimental data are captured by the coupled pore
model.

III. GRAIN CONSOLIDATION MODEL
The µGC model 29 is a simple periodic lattice of microporous
spherical grains, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Each grain is formed of
spherical nonporous particles uniformly distributed on a regular
sublattice. At the lattice or sublattice length scale, spherical parti-
cles of radius R are arranged on a simple grid [Fig. 1(a)] to deﬁne
the cubic unit cell of side length A shown in Fig. 1(b). At the
lattice scale, the porous grains have radius Rm and the unit cell
has a side length Am; at the sublattice scale, the solid particles
have radius Rµ and the unit cell has a side length Aµ. The ratio
Am/Aµ ∼100.
A. Random walk simulations
Random walk simulations were performed directly in the µGC
geometry using a variable time step (ﬁrst arrival) technique. 36,37
Walkers (nuclear spins) were seeded over the entire unit cell, and
those assigned to the solid volume were rejected. The ratio of
accepted to rejected walkers provided an estimate of the porosity.
Walkers diffused to a solid surface with an arrival time determined
by an analytic probability function. Once a walker was within a dis-
tance 3 of a solid surface, its motion was governed by ﬁxed jumps
of length  = 0.01 µm. When in proximity to the surface, the walker
had a probability γ of relaxing, determined by the surface relaxivity

C. Finite element simulations

physics drawing tools in 3D (Fig. 1) or 1D (Fig. 2). The model
was divided into two domains corresponding to the macropore and

VI. VISUALIZING EXCHANGE
When inspecting the relaxation time distributions in Figs. 4(a)
and 6(a), for example, one can make the naïve assignment of peaks in
the bi-modal distributions to nuclear spins in the micropores (short
relaxation time) and macropores (long relaxation time). However,
this is incorrect as the ratio of the peak areas does not match the
porosity fractions of the micropores and macropores, as demon-
strated previously. 3 Diffusion of magnetization between pores on
the time scale of the measurement alters the position and ampli-
tude of the observed components. To inspect the inﬂuence of this
diffusive coupling, we can adjust the input parameters of our sim-
ulation and initialize the model with magnetization conﬁned in the
macropores or micropores. To visualize this exchange process, we
use the 1D geometry of Fig. 2. The ﬁnite element simulation allows
the initial spatial distribution of magnetization at t = 0 to be triv-
ially modiﬁed. Such modiﬁcations could be achieved in the analytic
solutions or random walk simulations but with signiﬁcantly more
effort. An example result is presented in Fig. 7 using the same input
parameters as the simulations in Fig. 4(a) but with m(x ≤Lm, 0)
= 1 (macropore domain) and m(x > Lm, 0) = 0 (grain domain) in
Fig. 7(a), and m(x≤Lm, 0) = 0 (macropore domain) andm(x> Lm, 0)
= 1 (grain domain) in Fig. 7(c). When the magnetization is present
initially in the macropore only [Fig. 7(a)], it diffuses a signiﬁcant
distance into the grain before relaxing. Despite this exchange pro-
cess, the relaxation time distribution in Fig. 7(b) contains a single
peak located close to Tm, corresponding to relaxation in the macro-
pore. Despite extensive diffusion of magnetization into the grain, the
microporous region acts only as a relaxing surface under conditions
of “fast diffusion,” so a single relaxation time is observed. When
the magnetization is present initially in the microporous grain only
[Fig. 7(c)], it diffuses out into the macropore. The magnetization in
the macropore increases at short time ( t → Tµ), before decreasing
again at long times (t → Tm). Now, the resulting relaxation time dis-
tribution in Fig. 7(d) contains two peaks. The majority of the signal
reﬂects the relaxation inside the grain, and the main peak is located
near Tµ. However, magnetization that has diffused into the macrop-
ore relaxes at the slower rate and a notable peak is present near Tm.
To conﬁrm these observations, the sum of the data in Figs. 7(a) and
7(c) is shown in Fig. 7(e), where the spatial variation in magnetiza-
tion is indistinguishable from the result in Fig. 5(a). Similarly, the
relaxation time distributions in Figs. 7(b) and 7(d) were summed to
give the distribution in Fig. 7(f). Also shown in Fig. 7(f) is the distri-
bution repeated from Fig. 7(a). The peak positions are identical, and
although the amplitudes appear different due to smoothing in the
regularization step, the integral areas match. This manipulation of
the system is a powerful tool for exploring the inﬂuence of exchange
on measured relaxation time distributions.

ments is observed in multidimensional relaxation time correlations.

VII. SIMULATION AND EXPERIMENT
Finally, we demonstrate the utility of ﬁnite element methods
by comparing experimental and simulated T1-T2 correlations. The
experimental results obtained on silica powders with a variety of
grain sizes are shown in Fig. 9 (left). When the grains were rela-
tively small, Rm = 3–17.5 µm in Fig. 9(a), rapid exchange between
the grain and macropores occurred on the time scale of the exper-
iments and a single dominant peak was observed. As the grain size
increased, through Figs. 9(c), 9(e), and 9(g), off-diagonal exchange
peaks were observed consistent with diffusive coupling between two
environments. In Fig. 9(e), two peaks are observed at long times near
the T1/T2 = 10 diagonal and these multiple components are associ-
ated with the broad distribution of grain sizes ( Rm = 30–100 µm) in
this sample. It is interesting to observe that in Figs. 9(a), 9(c), and
9(e), multiple peaks are visible only in the T2 marginal distribution
and the T1 marginal distribution is dominated by a single relaxation
time component. This bias is attributed to details of the exchange
process, where the off-diagonal peak at T1 /uni226BT2 is expected to have
a larger amplitude than the off-diagonal peak at T2 /uni226AT1. The rel-
atively low amplitude of the peaks at short relaxation times visible
in Figs. 9(c) and 9(e) is attributed to weak surface relaxation in the
microporous grain. The distribution in Fig. 9(g) is also interesting
for its complexity. The off-diagonal exchange peaks are well deﬁned
and have signiﬁcant and similar magnitude (albeit opposite sign).
A broad, multimodal, and almost continuous distribution stretches
diagonally across the plot, extending over two orders of magnitude
in T2. This relaxation behavior is attributed to the range of grain
sizes combined with “intermediate” diffusion in the macropores.
The distribution shifts to a lowerT1/T2 ratio at the longest relaxation
times due to the inﬂuence of the bulk liquid relaxation, estimated as
T1b ≈T2b = 2 s for de-ionized water measured on a low-ﬁeld NMR
instrument. Overall, the relatively long relaxation times combined
with small micropore size suggest weak surface relaxation, and a
ratio of ρ2/ρ1∼10. Such a high ratio of surface relaxivities is uncom-
mon in coupled porous media such as reservoir rocks 51 where sur-
face relaxation is dominated by paramagnetic moieties. 52 However,
in the absence of strong relaxation sinks, the surface relaxation is
controlled by modiﬁed molecular dynamics 53 and high ρ2/ρ1 ratios
have been observed previously on clean oxide surfaces, such as
catalyst supports.54

VIII. CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we introduced ﬁnite element models of NMR
measurements in coupled pore systems. For simplicity, we utilized
the COMSOL Multiphysics software to solve the Bloch-Torrey equa-
tions for diffusing magnetization. The models were designed to
reﬂect granular porous media consisting of spherical microporous
grains separated by macroscopic interstices (macropores). Such
materials include carbonate rocks, cements, catalyst supports, and
biological cells. To validate these models, we based our simulations
on an existing microporous grain consolidation ( µGC) geometry.29
In order to successfully mesh the geometries, it was necessary to rep-
resent the microporous grains as an effective medium with modiﬁed
diffusion and relaxation properties. The ﬁnite element simulations
in an analog 1D geometry were shown to be equivalent to analytic
solutions, and the simulations in the 3D µGC model were compara-
ble to random walk simulations. The ﬁnite element simulations did
not capture the surface roughness of the grains present in the ran-
dom walk simulations due to the use of a smooth interface between
the macropore and microporous grain, but this detail could be rep-
resented by adjusted interface boundary conditions. Nevertheless,
the salient features of (a) diffusive coupling and (b) Brownstein-Tarr
“slow diffusion” in the macropores when the surface relaxivity was
strong were reproduced correctly in the ﬁnite element simulations.
Finally, we used the 3D simulations to replicate experimental results
of water-saturated silica powder and hence estimate the longitudinal
and transverse surface relaxivity parameters that are otherwise chal-
lenging to determine in the presence of diffusive coupling between
relaxation environments.
The ﬁnite element simulations offer several advantages over
existing forward modeling techniques to improve the interpretation
of NMR relaxation time measurements in coupled porous media.
Arbitrary grain structures can be constructed, or formed from µ-CT
images of real materials, whereas the existing analytic solutions are
limited to simple geometric forms. The ﬁnite element simulations
solve the spatial distribution of magnetization directly, allowing
convenient visualization of the time-dependent diffusion processes,
even in 3D geometries. Modiﬁcations of the input ﬁnite element
simulation parameters are trivial, such as the initial distribution of
magnetization, allowing exchange processes to be explored. These
simulations are also readily extensible to multidimensional NMR
experiments, such as T1-T2 or T2-T2 correlations.
