Experimental Identification of Diffusive Coupling Using 2D NMR
Y .-Q. Song,1* G. Carneiro, 1,2 L. M. Schwartz, 1 and D. L. Johnson 1
1Schlumberger-Doll Research, One Hampshire Street, Cambridge, Massachusetts 02139, USA
2Schlumberger Brazil Research and Geoengineering Center, Cidade Universitaria, Rio de Janeiro 21941-907, Brazil
(Received 30 March 2014; published 4 December 2014)
Spin relaxation based nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) methods have been used extensively to
determine pore size distributions in a variety of materials. This approach is based on the assumption that
each pore is in the fast diffusion limit but that diffusion between pores can be neglected. However, in
complex materials these assumptions may be violated and the relaxation time distribution is not easily
interpreted. We present a 2D NMR technique and an associated data analysis that allow us to work directly
with the time dependent experimental data without Laplace inversion to identify the signature of diffusive
coupling between different pores. Measurements on microporous glass beads and numerical simulations
are used to illustrate the technique.

NMR has been used to study a wide array of biological,
geological, and synthetic porous materials [1,2]. In these
systems the decay of the spin magnetization is controlled
by a combination of diffusion and enhanced relaxation at
the pore-solid interface. If the surface relaxation is rela-
tively weak, then each pore will exhibit single exponential
decay with a lifetime determined by its size; this is the fast
diffusion regime, and the net decay signal is a sum of
exponentials that reflects the distribution of pore sizes.
However, these assumptions can break down, either
because the surface relaxation is large enough that relax-
ation within a given pore involves several modes or because
interpore diffusion is important. For example, in materials
with a mixture of small and large pores, the 1D relaxation
time spectrum can be either very narrow or very broad,
depending on the connectivity between pores of different
sizes [3]. The situation is further complicated by the
presence of spatial heterogeneity of pore sizes over dis-
tances beyond the diffusion length (typically ∼100 μm) [4].
This Letter presents a 2D NMR relaxation method to
identify the diffusion regimes.
Two-dimensional NMR experiments involve a correlated
measurement of longitudinal ( T
1) and transverse ( T2)
relaxation and a 2D correlation map (e.g., T1-T2)i s
obtained via a 2D Laplace inversion [5–9]. In these
experiments diffusive coupling and pore size distributions
can exhibit qualitatively different features. For example,
when all of the pores are in the fast diffusion regime, the
signal from each pore will exhibit a single T
1 and T2 value
and thus appears as a single point on the 2D correlation
plot. A distribution of pore sizes will lead to signals along
a ridge in the 2D plot with the specific ratio r ≡ T1=T2.
On the other hand, if the fast diffusion regime does not
apply, the 2D T1-T2 cross plot can be considerably more
complicated with significant contributions away from the
T2 ¼ rT1 ridge. Indeed, it has been shown theoretically
that in certain regions the signal can have small negative
amplitude peaks, a fact that severely limits the stability of
the 2D Laplace inversion [7,10,11]. Therefore, a new
approach to the T1-T2 measurement is needed for complex
systems. We introduce a way of working directly with the
time-domain data that can sense the small negative signals
that are associated with more complex diffusion phenom-
ena. This technique is illustrated with a study of packed
microporous glass beads where diffusion occurs between
the intergranular pores and the micropores inside the grains.
Our measurements and numerical simulations of the
diffusion dynamics are shown to identify the presence of
the negative correlation peaks. Our experiments were
exclusively performed at low magnetic field ( ∼500 G) to
avoid the complication of higher magnetic fields [12].

T1-T2 measurements were carried out on a water
saturated pack (1 in. diameter and 1 in. long) of about
150 μm microporous beads (CPG3000A, Millipore), and
on a bulk water sample for comparison (Fig. 3). The CPG
beads are themselves porous, with an average micropore
diameter of 0.3 μm. For this sample the total porosity is
80% and is divided equally between the micro- and
macropores. The NMR experiments were performed using
a Rock Core Analyzer (Magritek Limited, Wellington, NZ)
at
1H NMR frequency of 2 MHz. Because of the large
surface area, spin relaxation for the water inside the grains
is substantially enhanced. The water molecules in the
macropores (between the grains) are relaxed by diffusion
into the micropores. Because diffusion between the micro-
pores and the macropores is an essential part of the overall
relaxation process, this system cannot be described with a
picture based on fast diffusion.

this behavior is a clear indication of negative peaks in

To further validate our argument, we have performed
numerical simulations based on a three-dimensional micro-
porous model whose geometrical parameters can be
matched to those of the bead pack discussed in connection
with Fig. 3. The simulations employ variable step-size
random walks in a packing of microporous spherical grains
[16,17]. The large grains are arranged on a face centered
cubic lattice; by adjusting their radius we can fix the
amount of macroporosity at 0.40. We have examined two
ways of representing the microporous structure within these
grains based on periodic arrangements of either spherical
micrograins or overlapping spherical micropores. In both
cases the porosity within the large grains is adjusted to 0.67
so that the porosity contribution from the macro- and
micropores are both 0.40. The grain sizes are adjusted
so that the effective pore volume to surface area ratio,
V
P=S ¼ 0.31 μm. The diffusion coefficient for the bulk
fluid is 2.5 μm2=ms. First, the comparison of the measured
diffusion coefficient [ DðtÞ] with the simulation [16],
Fig. 5(a), shows that the model with spherical micropores
provides a much better fit to the experimental diffusion
data. Second, the simulation of the T
1 and T2 distributions
is compared with our experiment [Fig. 5(b)] in order to
determine ρ1 and ρ2. Next, the micropore model was used
to simulate the T1-T2 experiment and the results are
presented in Fig. 5(c). The calculations are presented in
the same fashion as the experimental data [Fig. 3(b)] and a
small positive slope is observed for τ2 > 240 ms, as in the
experiment.
Consistency between our measurements, simulation,
and analysis demonstrates that the T1-T2 experiment can
identify deviations from the fast diffusion regime. Evidence
for this effect can be seen directly in the time-domain data
without the complication of the Laplace inversion. In the
presence of a broad spectrum of pore sizes, this method
should continue to work well because SðT
1;T 2Þ integrates
contributions from different pore sizes and the negative
contributions will remain. The position of the negative
structure is likely to depend on the pore size and will
produce a strip of off-diagonal negative amplitude (instead
of individual peaks). The location and magnitude of this
structure will hold the information about pore coupling in
such systems.
In conclusion, we have demonstrated that time depen-
dent 2D NMR data can be used directly to identify complex
diffusion regimes and diffusive coupling between pores
in porous media. We plan to extend our analysis to other
2D experiments (e.g., T
2-storage-T2 experiments) [7,17]
and natural samples with a wide distribution of pore sizes.
