abstract
Low ﬁeld NMR T2 distribution is an efﬁcient tool for characterizing pore size distribution in porous media.
In NMR relaxation experiments, we measure the magnetization decay characterized by the so-called T2
relaxation time, resulting essentially from liquid–solid interactions of the spins carried by molecules
and exploring the pore space by diffusion. For multimodal systems, a new technique called T2-store-T2
allows the analysis of diffusional pore to pore exchange that is extremely useful for the characterization
of connectivity. This technique uses 2D inverse Laplace techniques and produces T
2–T2 maps. Qualita-
tively, a system is coupled when off-diagonal peaks are observed.
Based on an analytical solution describing diffusional coupling between two pore populations, we pro-
pose deﬁning a coupling factor that quantify the degree of coupling between the two populations, pro-
viding an easy understanding of the complex analytical solution. This analysis allows understanding
1D T
2 experiments as well, and indicates some limitations of the T2 characterization when interpreted
as a pore size distribution.
We provide examples of bimodal pore structures in which we applied our methodology: a clay gel sys-
tem, a shaly sandstone and two double porosity carbonates. These systems are also described by conven-
tional techniques (mercury injection, SEM visualization) and illustrate weak, intermediate and strong
coupling. Despite the presence of distribution of pore sizes, the two pore system exchange model gives
satisfactory results for the quantitative analysis of the coupling and T
2-store-T2 experiments. In carbon-
ates, more complex exchanges can occur between micro-, meso- and macropores.

1. Introduction
Low ﬁeld NMR relaxation is a unique non-invasive and non-
destructive technique to characterize pore size distribution of por-
ous media. Spin bearing molecules explore the pore space by diffu-
sion and provide a unique information about the volume to surface
ratio of a given compartment. This principle was ﬁrst evidenced by
Brownstein and Tarr [1] in biological cells and relies on a contrast
between surface and bulk relaxivity. It is used extensively for the
interpretation of NMR relaxation data in natural porous media
especially in the ﬁeld of petrophysics. Without considering the
additional difﬁculty of the fast and slow diffusion regimes of relax-
ation [2], i.e. we consider typical pore sizes and surface relaxivity
values, the pore size interpretation is thought to be usually valid
because the diffusion length during the lifetime of the magnetiza-
tion is of the same order of the distance between grains. Hence, the
exchange of molecules between pores should be limited. However,
there are situations where this is not the case.
Diffusional pore coupling is an issue of practical importance,
especially in the ﬁeld of logging interpretation. NMR relaxation
data should not depend on temperature and this fact was put for-
ward to justify the surface dominated regime [3]. However, in car-
bonates, a signiﬁcant temperature dependence can occur, typically
when comparing laboratory and log data performed, respectively,
at 20 and 100 /C176C. This is a direct effect of pore coupling and a solu-
tion to predict the shift of the T
2 distributions was proposed [4].A t
the extreme situation, a multimodal system may appear unimodal
and this has important interpretation consequences since the NMR
log is the only in situ measurement sensitive to the pore structure.
Later, Anand et al. [5,6] proposed a 1D geometric model and a cou-
pling parameter to describe the pore to pore coupling phenomenon
and applied it to various situations. For bimodal pore systems, the
shift of macro- and micropore NMR peaks can be predicted as a
function of the coupling parameter comparing the characteristic
relaxation rate to the diffusion rate.
From the NMR point of view, the methods for observing the
modiﬁcation of the environment of spin carrying molecules has
been evidenced very early in the development of NMR [7]. At high
ﬁeld, the analysis of the chemical exchange is now a common prac-
tice, for example in the exchange spectroscopy technique EXSY [8]

tens of microns. We study the pore to pore exchange in a natural
bimodal model system composed of ﬂocculated clay particles,
and samples typical of oil bearing rocks: a high permeability sand-
stone containing clays and two complex carbonates.

Experiments were performed on a MARAN-ULTRA 23.7 MHz from

samples are small cylinders of diameter 15 mm and length
20 mm. For the gels, we used a glass container of similar size.

4.3. Double porosity carbonates
Carbonates often contain multiple pore sizes due to various dia-
genetic processes. Contrary to sandstones, natural carbonates are
made of various debris (animals, plants) that are subjected to dis-
solution/precipitation before, during or after burial. Hence, poros-
ity can be present between, as well as, inside ‘‘grains”. The
samples used here are part of a larger study comparing NMR T2 dis-
tributions and pore throat size distributions deduced from mercury
injection experiments (MICP), with the help of visual examination
of the pore structure from thin sections and scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) [16,17]. In this work, it was established that
microporosity deﬁned in the petrographical sense, is the volume
accessible through throat diameters smaller than 2
lm and corre-
spond to relaxation times smaller than 200 ms in the T2 distribu-
tion. An example is given in Fig. 13 . Macroporosity is located
between red algaes, that are themselves porous. As a result, the
MICP distribution indicates a bimodal distribution. The NMR T
2
distribution indicates similarly a bimodal distribution shifted, as
expected, to the left of the MICP distribution, after calculation of
the surface relaxivity from BET measurements. The macropore
peak is centered around T
2 = 1000 ms, and we performed a 2D
experiment using an exchange time of te = 1000 ms that indicated
no off-diagonal peak and therefore no pore to pore diffusional ex-
change. The presence of sparitic cement as well as a cement rim
around red algae is the most likely explanation for the lack of cou-
pling. Also, the size of the object containing microporosity is large
(300
lm) compared to the diffusion length of molecules during the
exchange time (about 100 lm for 1000 ms).
A careful observation of the porous structure may give good
indication of the existence of pore coupling. In the case of the Lav-

surface. In this case, the bimodal MICP signature (Fig. 14 , right) is
in contradiction with the 1D NMR when saturated with water

100 lm) that should have a large relaxation time (1000 ms) similar

5. Conclusion
Diffusional coupling is a very common mechanism, potentially
perturbing the interpretation of 1D relaxation time distributions,
but providing new information of the connectivity of porous sys-
tems. For bimodal systems in which we cover a range of pore sizes
from 1 nm up to 100
lm, we deﬁned a coupling parameter a com-
paring the average exchange rate and the relaxation rate of the
smallest pore population (micropore) having the largest speciﬁc
surface. Based on an analytical solution, we analyzed the behavior
of 1D T
2 and 2D T2-store-T2 relaxation experiments as a function of
a. In 1D experiments and for increasing values of a, the relaxation
time of micropores is shifted to smaller values and their amplitude
decreases. The coupling parameter, which is proportional to the
diffusivity of the saturating ﬂuid, must vary by two orders of mag-
nitude to switch from a weakly to a strongly coupled state. For 2D
T2-store-T2 experiments, diagonal peak intensities are maximum at
intermediate coupling degrees and represent at maximum 10% of
the total intensity for usual T
1/T2 ratio of 1.5. Therefore, these
experiments require high signal to noise ratio, of the order of
200 or larger, and a careful use of 2D inverse Laplace transform,
as illustrated by various tests of the inversion process. The cou-
pling was evidenced and quantiﬁed on a ﬂocculated smectite gel
and a shaly sandstone. Although relatively simple, the two pore
population model allows the analysis of distributed bimodal sys-
tems. On complex carbonate structures, multiple exchanges were
observed and explained with the help of SEM visualizations but
the two pore population model could not be used for a quantitative
analysis of molecular exchange. We suggest that this difﬁculty is
either due a distribution of exchange times or the partial exchange
of macropore water with microporosity in the case of large pores.
An estimate of the order of magnitude of the degree is performed
by changing the diffusivity of the saturating liquid by a factor of
ten.

It is usually necessary to have small s values to properly

number of pulses when large and small relaxation times are

limit the total number of pulses to about 10,000 (to avoid a po-

relaxation time present in the sample). Then, it stays at a con-
stant value. During the second CPMG sequence, typically 5000
echo maximum are recorded (the value of n is tuned such as
to obtain a signal decreasing to zero, as usually performed in a
single CPMG sequence). Then this signal is compressed by aver-
aging all echo maximum in 40 predeﬁned bins having a constant
log spacing in time (with origin the third P90), yielding a com-
pressed time series tc. For the ﬁrst CPMG sequence, only the m
values corresponding to the 40 values of tc are used. Hence,
the two-dimensional data set acquired reduces ﬁnally to a
40 /C2 40 matrix. The advantage of this protocol is the large in-
crease of signal to noise ratio for intermediate and large relaxa-
tion times due to compression.
